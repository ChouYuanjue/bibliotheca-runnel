\notechapter{N-20230310}{From Tangent Lines to Convex Duality and the Geometry of \(L^p\)}

A derivative is a local linear approximation. For a differentiable function \(f\), the tangent approximation at \(a\) is
\[
 f(a+h)=f(a)+f'(a)h+o(|h|).
\]
When \(f\) is convex, that local line provides a \emph{global} lower bound. This observation is the route from elementary calculus to Young's inequality, then to Holder's and Minkowski's inequalities, and finally to the duality between \(L^p\) norms. Our convex-analytic conventions follow Boyd and Vandenberghe, \emph{Convex Optimization}, Section 3.3, together with the \(L^p\) material in MIT 18.102.\footnote{Boyd and Vandenberghe, \href{https://web.stanford.edu/~boyd/cvxbook/}{\emph{Convex Optimization}}, Chapter 3; MIT 18.102, functional-analysis lecture notes.}

\section{What Tangent Lines Remember}

The derivative begins as a limit of secant slopes,
\[
 f'(a)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}h,
\]
and differentiability means
\[
 f(a+h)=f(a)+f'(a)h+r(h),\qquad
 \lim_{h\to0}\frac{r(h)}{|h|}=0.
\]
The linear term is the best first-order local description of the function. For a twice differentiable function, the next term is
\[
 f(a+h)=f(a)+f'(a)h+\frac12f''(a)h^2+o(h^2).
\]
This expansion reveals why the usual first-derivative test looks for changes in the sign of $f'$ and the second-derivative test looks for the curvature $f''$: both are ways of detecting what first survives when the leading term disappears.

One should not silently identify a stationary point with an extremum. The function $f(x)=x^3$ has $f'(0)=0$, but takes both signs on either side of zero. Meanwhile $f(x)=|x|$ has a strict minimum at zero \emph{without} having a derivative there. Fermat's theorem says that differentiability plus an \emph{interior extremum} implies $f'=0$; the converse need not hold, and nondifferentiable extrema are not excluded.

Convexity adds something much stronger. For a differentiable convex function and $0<t<1$,
\[
 f(x+t(y-x))\le(1-t)f(x)+tf(y).
\]
Rearranging, dividing by $t>0$, and letting $t\downarrow0$ gives
\[
 f'(x)(y-x)\le f(y)-f(x).
\]
So the tangent line at $x$ is a \emph{global} supporting line. This is exactly the inequality used below to derive Young's inequality. Convexity converts local slope data into an everywhere-valid comparison.

\section{Convexity turns a tangent into an inequality}

A differentiable function \(f\) on an interval is convex if and only if its graph lies above every tangent:
\[
 \boxed{f(y)\ge f(x)+f'(x)(y-x).}
\]
For a twice differentiable function, \(f''\ge0\) guarantees this property. Indeed, by the mean value theorem applied to \(f'\), its derivative is nondecreasing, so the secant slopes to the right of \(x\) are no smaller than \(f'(x)\); the same estimate follows on the left.

The simple convex function \(f(t)=e^t\) satisfies \(e^t\ge1+t\) by taking the tangent at \(0\). For \(f(t)=-\log t\) on \(t>0\), the tangent at \(1\) gives
\[
 -\log t\ge1-t,\qquad\text{or}\qquad \log t\le t-1.
\]
These are global inequalities obtained from a first-order local calculation. Equality holds at the tangency point.

\section{Young's inequality is a conjugate-function calculation}

Let \(p>1\) and \(q=p/(p-1)\). Consider the convex function
\[
 \phi(u)=\frac{|u|^p}{p}.
\]
For each real \(v\), its convex conjugate is
\[
 \phi^*(v)=\sup_{u\in\mathbb R}\{uv-\phi(u)\}.
\]
The optimizing \(u\) has the sign of \(v\), and the stationarity equation is
\[
 |u|^{p-1}=|v|.
\]
Thus \(u=\operatorname{sgn}(v)|v|^{q-1}\), and substitution gives
\[
 \boxed{\phi^*(v)=\frac{|v|^q}{q}.}
\]
By the definition of a supremum, \(uv-\phi(u)\le\phi^*(v)\). We obtain Young's inequality
\[
 \boxed{uv\le\frac{|u|^p}{p}+\frac{|v|^q}{q}.}
\]
Equality holds exactly when \(v=\operatorname{sgn}(u)|u|^{p-1}\).

This explains the conjugate exponents more sharply than memorizing \(1/p+1/q=1\). The power \(q\) appears because the inverse of \(u\mapsto |u|^{p-1}\operatorname{sgn}(u)\) is \(v\mapsto |v|^{q-1}\operatorname{sgn}(v)\).

\section{Holder's inequality is Young after normalization}

For nonnegative numbers \(a_i,b_i\), put
\[
 A=\left(\sum_i a_i^p\right)^{1/p},\qquad
 B=\left(\sum_i b_i^q\right)^{1/q}.
\]
If \(A=0\) or \(B=0\), the inequality below is immediate. Otherwise Young's inequality applied to \(a_i/A\) and \(b_i/B\) gives
\[
 \frac{a_ib_i}{AB}
 \le\frac{a_i^p}{pA^p}+\frac{b_i^q}{qB^q}.
\]
Sum over \(i\). The right-hand side becomes \(1/p+1/q=1\), yielding
\[
 \boxed{\sum_i a_ib_i\le
 \left(\sum_i a_i^p\right)^{1/p}
 \left(\sum_i b_i^q\right)^{1/q}.}
\]
For arbitrary signed or complex sequences, replace each product by its absolute value. Exactly the same pointwise argument, with integration in place of summation, proves
\[
 \int|fg|\le\|f\|_{L^p}\|g\|_{L^q}
\]
whenever the respective norms are finite.

There is a revealing dual formulation in finite-dimensional real spaces (for complex vectors, use the absolute value of the Hermitian pairing):
\[
 \boxed{\|x\|_p=\sup_{\|y\|_q\le1}\sum_i x_i y_i.}
\]
Holder gives the upper bound. If \(x\ne0\), choose
\[
 y_i=\frac{\operatorname{sgn}(x_i)|x_i|^{p-1}}{\|x\|_p^{p-1}}.
\]
Then \(\|y\|_q=1\), and \(\sum_i x_iy_i=\|x\|_p\). The dual vector is exactly the equality case of Young's inequality, now interpreted as the linear measurement achieving the norm.

\section[Minkowski and the p-norm]{Minkowski makes \(\ell^p\) into a normed space}

For \(p>1\), let \(z=x+y\). By Holder with exponents \(p,q\),
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \|z\|_p^p
 &=\sum_i|z_i|^p\\
 &\le\sum_i |x_i||z_i|^{p-1}
       +\sum_i|y_i||z_i|^{p-1}\\
 &\le(\|x\|_p+\|y\|_p)
       \left(\sum_i|z_i|^{(p-1)q}\right)^{1/q}\\
 &=(\|x\|_p+\|y\|_p)\|z\|_p^{p-1}.
\end{aligned}
\]
If \(z=0\), the conclusion is trivial; otherwise divide by \(\|z\|_p^{p-1}\) to obtain
\[
 \boxed{\|x+y\|_p\le\|x\|_p+\|y\|_p.}
\]
The same reasoning applies to \(L^p\) integrals, while \(p=1\) follows directly from the pointwise triangle inequality.

There is a conceptual difference between proving an inequality and proving a norm axiom. Holder controls the pairing of vectors from conjugate exponent spaces; Minkowski uses that control to show that \(\ell^p\) is closed under addition with a subadditive notion of size. The norm's geometry is therefore built from its duality inequality.

\section{Why the exponent threshold really matters}

Young's inequality and its convex-conjugate proof require $p>1$, or $p=1$ with a limiting dual exponent $q=\infty$. What happens if we blindly extend the expression
\[
 \|x\|_p=\left(\sum_i|x_i|^p\right)^{1/p}
\]
to $0<p<1$? The expression is homogeneous and still vanishes only at zero, but it can fail the triangle inequality.

For example, take $p=1/2$ and
\[
 x=(1,0),\qquad y=(0,1).
\]
Then
\[
 \|x\|_{1/2}=\|y\|_{1/2}=1,\qquad
 \boxed{\|x+y\|_{1/2}=(1+1)^2=4>2.}
\]
The unit ball for this \emph{quasi-norm} is not convex. The proof of Minkowski breaks at precisely the same place: one would need the conjugate exponent $q=p/(p-1)<0$, and the convexity argument for the power function no longer applies.

The equality case of Holder is also geometric. When $p=q=2$, it reduces to Cauchy--Schwarz; equality for nonzero real vectors means that the vectors are proportional. The resulting Minkowski triangle inequality is strict for two nonzero vectors pointing in genuinely different directions. For $x=(1,0)$ and $y=(0,1)$,
\[
 \|x+y\|_2=\sqrt2<2=\|x\|_2+\|y\|_2,
\]
whereas if $y=tx$ with $t\ge0$ the triangle inequality becomes equality. This is why $p$-norms are not merely formulas for adding powers: their convexity properties determine the geometry of the unit ball and which inequalities may be used.

\section{Jensen's inequality and entropy}

For a convex function \(f\), nonnegative weights \(\alpha_i\) with \(\sum_i\alpha_i=1\) satisfy
\[
 f\left(\sum_i\alpha_i x_i\right)\le\sum_i\alpha_i f(x_i).
\]
This is Jensen's inequality, obtained by repeatedly applying the two-point definition of convexity. The direction reverses for concave functions such as \(\log\).

A particularly useful application involves probability vectors \(p=(p_i)\), \(q=(q_i)\) with strictly positive coordinates and total mass \(1\). Concavity of \(\log\) gives
\[
 \sum_i p_i\log\frac{q_i}{p_i}
 \le\log\left(\sum_i p_i\frac{q_i}{p_i}\right)
 =\log\sum_iq_i=0.
\]
Therefore the relative entropy, or Kullback--Leibler divergence, satisfies
\[
 \boxed{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)
       =\sum_i p_i\log\frac{p_i}{q_i}\ge0.}
\]
Equality, by strict concavity, holds if and only if \(p=q\). The result extends to zeros by the standard conventions and appropriate support conditions.

Taking \(q\) uniform on \(N\) points gives
\[
 H(p)=-\sum_i p_i\log p_i\le\log N,
\]
with equality at the uniform distribution. If there are \(2^n\) states and base-two logarithms are used, this reads \(H(p)\le n\).

The connection to entropy emerges from precisely the same tangent and convexity technology that produced Young's inequality. Convexity controls a function by linear data; conjugacy controls a bilinear pairing; Jensen controls averages. These three operations provide a common analytic language for norms, optimization, and information.
