\notechapter{N-20230123}{From Work Integrals to Phase-Space Area and Action Variables}

An integral measures accumulation, but the geometry of what is accumulated matters. Work is integrated along an oriented path, and the same line-integral mechanism reappears when one measures the area enclosed by a periodic trajectory in phase space. In that setting the integral \(\oint p\,dq\) turns out to determine the oscillation period without solving the equation of motion explicitly. This is a concrete route from first-year calculus to Hamiltonian mechanics.\footnote{MIT 18.02 (line integrals and Green's theorem); \href{https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-09-classical-mechanics-iii-fall-2014/pages/lecture-notes/}{MIT 8.09, Classical Mechanics III} (action variables); V. I. Arnold, \emph{Mathematical Methods of Classical Mechanics}.}

\section{Work is the accumulation of force along a path}

In one dimension, when a force \(F(x)\) acts while a particle moves from \(a\) to \(b\),
\[
 W=\int_a^bF(x)\,dx.
\]
For example, the spring force \(F(x)=-kx\) performs work
\[
 W_{a\to b}=-\frac k2(b^2-a^2).
\]
This calculation naturally suggests the potential energy \(U(x)=kx^2/2\), for which \(F=-U'\) and \(W=U(a)-U(b)\).

For a curve \(\gamma(t)=(x(t),y(t),z(t))\) in space, the displacement is \(d\mathbf r=\gamma'(t)\,dt\). The signed work becomes
\[
 \boxed{W_\gamma=\int_\gamma F\cdot d\mathbf r
 =\int_a^bF(\gamma(t))\cdot\gamma'(t)\,dt.}
\]
It depends on the path's orientation. If \(F=-\nabla U\), the chain rule gives
\[
 F(\gamma(t))\cdot\gamma'(t)
 =-\frac d{dt}U(\gamma(t)),
\]
so
\[
 \boxed{W_\gamma=U(\gamma(a))-U(\gamma(b)).}
\]
For a conservative force, the work depends only on the endpoints and vanishes around every closed loop.

\section{Circulation converts a line integral to area}

Consider the planar field \(F=(-y,x)\), which rotates counterclockwise around the origin. On a circle of radius \(R\),
\[
 \gamma(t)=(R\cos t,R\sin t),\quad0\le t\le2\pi,
\]
one obtains
\[
 \int_\gamma F\cdot d\mathbf r
 =\int_0^{2\pi}R^2\,dt=2\pi R^2.
\]
The curl of this field is constant:
\[
 \frac{\partial F_y}{\partial x}
 -\frac{\partial F_x}{\partial y}=2.
\]
Green's theorem therefore explains the result as
\[
 \oint_{\partial D}(-y\,dx+x\,dy)
 =\iint_D2\,dx\,dy=2\,\operatorname{area}(D).
\]
The calculation is exact for any sufficiently regular positively oriented planar region \(D\), not just circles.

Choosing instead the one-form \(x\,dy\), whose exterior derivative is \(dx\wedge dy\), gives the particularly simple area formula
\[
 \boxed{\operatorname{area}(D)=\oint_{\partial D}x\,dy
       =-\oint_{\partial D}y\,dx.}
\]
A planar region can therefore be measured by integrating around its boundary. This is the bridge from accumulation along a path to geometric area.

\section{The canonical one-form on phase space}

Now consider a particle moving in one dimension, with position \(q\), momentum \(p=m\dot q\), and mechanical energy
\[
 H(q,p)=\frac{p^2}{2m}+V(q).
\]
The pair \((q,p)\) describes a point in \emph{phase space}. A periodic mechanical motion traces a closed curve there. Along this curve, define the integral
\[
 \boxed{J(E)=\oint_{H=E}p\,dq.}
\]
Unlike the mechanical work \(\int F\,dq\), this integral measures momentum against displacement. Its units are momentum times length, or action.

The one-form \(\vartheta=p\,dq\) is the canonical one-form in these coordinates. Its differential is
\[
 d\vartheta=dp\wedge dq=-dq\wedge dp.
\]
Green's theorem thus interprets \(J(E)\) as an oriented phase-space area. For the usual Hamiltonian evolution of a one-dimensional oscillation, the energy orbit is traversed clockwise in \((q,p)\)-coordinates; the integral \(J(E)\) equals the ordinary positive area enclosed by that orbit.

\section{Action of the Harmonic Oscillator}

Take \(V(q)=kq^2/2\) with \(\omega=\sqrt{k/m}\). The energy curve is
\[
 \frac{p^2}{2mE}+\frac{kq^2}{2E}=1.
\]
It is an ellipse with horizontal semiaxis
\(q_{\max}=\sqrt{2E/k}\) and vertical semiaxis
\(p_{\max}=\sqrt{2mE}\). Its area is
\[
 J(E)=\pi q_{\max}p_{\max}
     =\pi\sqrt{\frac{2E}{k}}\sqrt{2mE}
     =\boxed{\frac{2\pi E}{\omega}}.
\]

The same answer can be checked by following one orbit:
\[
 q(t)=A\cos\omega t,\qquad
 p(t)=-m\omega A\sin\omega t,
 \qquad E=\frac12m\omega^2A^2.
\]
Since \(dq/dt=-A\omega\sin\omega t\),
\[
 \oint p\,dq
 =m\omega^2A^2\int_0^{2\pi/\omega}\sin^2\omega t\,dt
 =\pi m\omega A^2=\frac{2\pi E}{\omega}.
\]
The boundary-integral interpretation and the direct time integral are equivalent because the particle traces precisely the ellipse.

\section{Differentiating enclosed area recovers the period}

The oscillator identity is an instance of a general fact. Suppose
\[
 H(q,p)=\frac{p^2}{2m}+V(q)
\]
has a regular family of periodic energy orbits between simple turning points \(q_-(E)<q_+(E)\). On the upper half of the orbit,
\[
 p_+(q,E)=\sqrt{2m(E-V(q))},
\]
and the lower half has momentum \(-p_+\). The enclosed area is therefore
\[
 J(E)=2\int_{q_-(E)}^{q_+(E)}
            \sqrt{2m(E-V(q))}\,dq.
\]
Differentiating with respect to energy is legitimate away from critical energy levels. The integrand vanishes at the moving turning points, so their endpoint contributions vanish. We obtain
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \frac{dJ}{dE}
 &=2\int_{q_-}^{q_+}
       \frac{m}{\sqrt{2m(E-V(q))}}\,dq\\
 &=2\int_{q_-}^{q_+}\frac{dq}{\dot q}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Here \(\dot q=p/m>0\) on the upper branch. The last integral is twice the travel time from the left turning point to the right turning point, hence the full period:
\[
 \boxed{\frac{dJ}{dE}=T(E).}
\]
For the harmonic oscillator \(J(E)=2\pi E/\omega\), this immediately yields \(T=2\pi/\omega\), without solving \(m\ddot q+kq=0\).

The formula is useful for anharmonic potentials: the period's energy dependence is encoded in the changing area of the energy contours. If an energy approaches a separatrix, the period may diverge because motion slows near an unstable equilibrium; the derivative of the phase-space area captures that divergence.

\section{Canonical Coordinate Changes}

The phase-space area form is \(\omega=dq\wedge dp\). A canonical change of coordinates \((q,p)\mapsto(Q,P)\) preserves this two-form:
\[
 dq\wedge dp=dQ\wedge dP.
\]
Locally, the difference between the canonical one-forms is therefore closed:
\[
 d(p\,dq-P\,dQ)=0.
\]
On a simply connected coordinate patch it is exact, say \(p\,dq-P\,dQ=dS\). Around a closed orbit lying in such a patch,
\[
 \oint p\,dq-\oint P\,dQ=\oint dS=0.
\]
Consequently the action is a coordinate-invariant measure of the enclosed symplectic area. The requirement about the coordinate patch matters: a closed form on a multiply connected domain need not have a globally defined potential.

The path integral has now done three kinds of work. Integrating \(F\,dq\) gives the mechanical work performed by a force, integrating around the boundary of a planar region measures ordinary area, and integrating \(p\,dq\) around a periodic trajectory measures phase-space area. In a one-degree-of-freedom Hamiltonian system, differentiating this last area with respect to energy gives a directly observable quantity: the period.
