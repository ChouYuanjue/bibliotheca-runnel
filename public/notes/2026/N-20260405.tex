\notechapter{N-20260405}{Changing Variables in Double Integrals: Geometry, Jacobians, and Multiplicity}

The bounds of an iterated integral are descriptions of a region, not decorations attached to the integration symbols. A good coordinate change transforms a difficult region into a simple one, but it must also measure how area changes. The determinant of the derivative gives this local area factor. When the coordinate map covers a point several times, an additional global issue appears: multiplicity.\footnote{For Fubini and the multivariable change-of-variables theorem, see MIT 18.02SC, \emph{Multivariable Calculus}, Units 4--5: \url{https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-02sc-multivariable-calculus-fall-2010/}. The general multiplicity formula is discussed in Evans and Gariepy, \emph{Measure Theory and Fine Properties of Functions}.}

\section{A reversed integral should describe the same region}

Consider the region between \(y=x^2\) and \(y=x\) for \(0\le x\le1\). It is
\[
 D=\{(x,y):0\le x\le1,\ x^2\le y\le x\}.
\]
For each fixed \(x\), the vertical slice has length \(x-x^2\), so
\[
 \operatorname{area}(D)=\int_0^1(x-x^2)\,dx=\frac16.
\]
If we choose horizontal slices, the conditions \(x^2\le y\le x\) become
\[
 y\le x\le\sqrt y,\qquad0\le y\le1.
\]
Therefore the same area is
\[
 \boxed{\int_0^1\int_{x^2}^{x}1\,dy\,dx
      =\int_0^1\int_y^{\sqrt y}1\,dx\,dy=\frac16.}
\]
Nothing about the integrand changed. We merely described the same measurable set as a union of different one-dimensional fibers. The equality is an instance of Fubini's theorem.

\section{Jacobian as an Area Scale}

Let \(\Phi(u,v)=(x(u,v),y(u,v))\) be a continuously differentiable map. Near a point \((u_0,v_0)\), a tiny rectangle of sides \(du,dv\) is sent, to first order, to a parallelogram spanned by
\[
 \Phi_u\,du,\qquad\Phi_v\,dv.
\]
Its area is
\[
 \left|\det\begin{pmatrix}x_u&x_v\\y_u&y_v\end{pmatrix}\right|du\,dv.
\]
Thus the local area scale is the absolute Jacobian determinant
\[
 J_\Phi=\det D\Phi.
\]
For a one-to-one \(C^1\) coordinate change with nonzero Jacobian on a suitable region, the change-of-variables theorem reads
\[
 \boxed{\iint_{\Phi(U)}f(x,y)\,dx\,dy
 =\iint_U f(\Phi(u,v))|J_\Phi(u,v)|\,du\,dv.}
\]
The absolute value ensures that ordinary area is positive whether the coordinate transformation preserves or reverses orientation. For oriented differential forms, the sign of the determinant is retained instead.

\section{Coordinates adapted to two families of straight lines}

Suppose a region is bounded by lines of the form \(x+y=\text{constant}\) and \(x-y=\text{constant}\). Set
\[
 u=x+y,\qquad v=x-y.
\]
Then
\[
 x=\frac{u+v}{2},\qquad y=\frac{u-v}{2},\qquad
 \det\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}=-\frac12.
\]
Consider the parallelogram
\[
 D=\{(x,y):0\le x+y\le2,\quad0\le x-y\le1\}.
\]
It becomes the rectangle \(0\le u\le2,\ 0\le v\le1\). Its area is immediately
\[
 \operatorname{area}(D)=\int_0^2\int_0^1\frac12\,dv\,du=1.
\]
The same substitution simplifies nonconstant integrands. Since
\[
 x^2-y^2=(x+y)(x-y)=uv,
\]
we get
\[
 \boxed{\iint_D(x^2-y^2)\,dx\,dy
 =\frac12\int_0^2\int_0^1uv\,dv\,du=\frac12.}
\]
The region and the integrand become simple at the same time. This is the real criterion for a useful substitution.

\section{Polar coordinates derive a Gaussian integral}

The polar map is
\[
 \Phi(r,\theta)=(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta),\qquad
 r\ge0,\quad0\le\theta<2\pi.
\]
Its derivative has determinant
\[
 \det\begin{pmatrix}
 \cos\theta&-r\sin\theta\\
 \sin\theta&r\cos\theta
 \end{pmatrix}=r.
\]
Thus, away from the origin and the seam at \(\theta=0\), polar coordinates give
\[
 dx\,dy=r\,dr\,d\theta.
\]
The factor \(r\) is not arbitrary: angular displacements of size \(d\theta\) sweep arcs whose length is approximately \(r\,d\theta\).

A particularly elegant use is to evaluate
\[
 I=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2}\,dx.
\]
The integral is positive and finite. Tonelli's theorem gives
\[
 I^2=\iint_{\mathbb R^2}e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\,dx\,dy.
\]
Polar coordinates convert the radial Gaussian into a one-dimensional exponential:
\[
 I^2=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2}r\,dr\,d\theta
 =2\pi\cdot\frac12=\pi.
\]
Hence
\[
 \boxed{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2}\,dx=\sqrt\pi.}
\]
The two-dimensional detour solves a one-dimensional integral because the product of two Gaussian factors becomes rotationally symmetric.

\section{Global Overcounting by a Jacobian}

The usual formula needs a one-to-one parametrization, at least apart from negligible boundary sets. To see why, identify the plane with the complex numbers and consider
\[
 \Phi(z)=z^2.
\]
On the annulus \(A=\{z:1<|z|<2\}\), the map is smooth and its real Jacobian is
\[
 |J_\Phi(z)|=|\Phi'(z)|^2=4|z|^2.
\]
Its image is the annulus \(B=\{w:1<|w|<4\}\). But each point of \(B\) has \emph{two} preimages in \(A\), the two square roots.

If we blindly apply the one-to-one formula to the constant function \(1\), we obtain
\[
 \int_A4|z|^2\,dA
 =\int_0^{2\pi}\int_1^2 4r^3\,dr\,d\theta
 =30\pi.
\]
The actual area of \(B\) is
\[
 |B|=\pi(4^2-1^2)=15\pi.
\]
The integral with the Jacobian is twice the image area, exactly because the annulus is covered twice:
\[
 \boxed{\int_A|J_\Phi(z)|\,dA=2\,|\Phi(A)|.}
\]
More generally, the area formula for a smooth finite-to-one map involves the number of preimages of almost every image point. A nonzero local Jacobian guarantees local invertibility, but it does not guarantee global one-to-one behavior.

\section{Coarea counts fibers of a nonlinear function}

The same distinction appears when the new variable is a scalar function rather than a full coordinate system. For a sufficiently regular \(g:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R\), the coarea formula relates a planar integral to integrals over the level curves \(g^{-1}(t)\):
\[
 \int_{\mathbb R^2}h(x)|\nabla g(x)|\,dx
 =\int_{\mathbb R}
       \left(\int_{g^{-1}(t)}h(x)\,d\mathcal H^1(x)\right)\,dt.
\]
For \(g(x,y)=x^2+y^2\), the level \(g=t>0\) is a circle of radius \(\sqrt t\), and \(|\nabla g|=2\sqrt t\). Consequently a radial function \(\psi(g)\) has
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \int_{\mathbb R^2}\psi(x^2+y^2)\,dx\,dy
 &=\int_0^\infty\psi(t)
     \frac{2\pi\sqrt t}{2\sqrt t}\,dt\\
 &=\boxed{\pi\int_0^\infty\psi(t)\,dt}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Taking \(\psi(t)=e^{-t}\) recovers the Gaussian calculation.

The lesson extends from elementary region sketches to geometric measure theory. Reversing integration order changes the slicing map; the Jacobian measures local area distortion; the area formula accounts for how often a parametrization covers the image; and coarea integrates over the fibers of a nonlinear function. Before making a substitution, the decisive questions are what the new fibers look like, how they scale, and whether they overlap.
