\notechapter{N-20260617}{Fourier Series: Projection, Summation, and Regularity}

The Fourier series of a periodic function is simultaneously a set of coordinates, a way of reconstructing a signal, and a spectral decomposition of differentiation. These interpretations agree, but only if we distinguish three notions of convergence: convergence in mean square, pointwise convergence, and uniform convergence. A single function with a jump already forces the distinction.\footnote{The normalization, Fej\'er argument and applications follow Stein and Shakarchi, \href{https://books.google.com/books/about/Fourier_Analysis.html?id=FAOc24bTfGkC}{\emph{Fourier Analysis: An Introduction}}, Chapters 1--3.}

\section{Fourier coefficients are orthogonal coordinates}

Work on the circle \(\mathbb T=\mathbb R/2\pi\mathbb Z\) with the normalized inner product
\[
 \langle f,g\rangle
 =\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}
     f(x)\overline{g(x)}\,dx.
\]
For \(n\in\mathbb Z\), put \(e_n(x)=e^{inx}\). Then
\[
 \langle e_m,e_n\rangle
 =\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{i(m-n)x}\,dx
 =\delta_{mn}.
\]
Thus the characters \(e_n\) form an orthonormal system; for \(f\in L^2(\mathbb T)\) define
\[
 \boxed{\widehat f(n)=\langle f,e_n\rangle
 =\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(x)e^{-inx}\,dx.}
\]
The truncated Fourier series is
\[
 S_Nf(x)=\sum_{|n|\le N}\widehat f(n)e^{inx}.
\]
It is the orthogonal projection of \(f\) onto the \((2N+1)\)-dimensional space of trigonometric polynomials of degree at most \(N\). Indeed, for every such polynomial \(P\),
\[
 \langle f-S_Nf,P\rangle=0,
\]
and Pythagoras gives the best-approximation identity
\[
 \boxed{\|f-P\|_2^2
 =\|f-S_Nf\|_2^2+\|S_Nf-P\|_2^2.}
\]
With this normalized inner product, the norm and Fourier coefficients will obey Parseval's formula without any extra \(2\pi\) factors.

\section{Fourier Coefficients versus Point Values}

Take \(f(x)=x\) on \((-\pi,\pi)\), extended \(2\pi\)-periodically. It is odd, so its cosine coefficients vanish. Integration by parts gives, for \(n\ge1\),
\[
\begin{aligned}
 b_n&=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi x\sin(nx)\,dx\\
 &=\frac2\pi\left[
 -\frac{x\cos(nx)}n+\frac{\sin(nx)}{n^2}
 \right]_0^\pi
 =\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}n.
\end{aligned}
\]
Consequently,
\[
 \boxed{x\sim2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}
                 \frac{(-1)^{n+1}}n\sin(nx).}
\]
For \(-\pi<x<\pi\), the series converges to \(x\). At \(x=\pi\), the periodic extension jumps from \(\pi\) to \(-\pi\); the series takes the midpoint \(0\), since every term is zero there.

The coefficients decay only like \(1/n\). This slow decay is a trace of the jump. Compare the continuous even function \(g(x)=|x|\). Again by integration by parts,
\[
 a_0=\pi,\qquad
 a_n=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi x\cos(nx)\,dx
     =\frac{2((-1)^n-1)}{\pi n^2}.
\]
Thus
\[
 \boxed{|x|=\frac\pi2-\frac4\pi
       \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}
       \frac{\cos((2k+1)x)}{(2k+1)^2}.}
\]
The corner has produced coefficients of order \(1/n^2\), and the series now converges uniformly by the Weierstrass test. Evaluating at \(x=0\) yields
\[
 \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac1{(2k+1)^2}
 =\frac{\pi^2}{8}.
\]
Since the odd terms form three quarters of \(\sum_{n\ge1}n^{-2}\), this also gives the Basel sum \(\sum_{n\ge1}n^{-2}=\pi^2/6\).

\section{The Dirichlet kernel explains both power and danger}

The partial sum has a convolution representation:
\[
 S_Nf(x)=\frac1{2\pi}
  \int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(x-t)D_N(t)\,dt,
\]
where
\[
 \boxed{D_N(t)=\sum_{n=-N}^{N}e^{int}
 =\frac{\sin((N+\tfrac12)t)}{\sin(t/2)}.}
\]
The integral of \(D_N\) is \(2\pi\), but it is not nonnegative, and its \(L^1\) norm grows on the order of \(\log N\). Ordinary partial sums are therefore not averages with positive weights. Oscillation can help reconstruct a function at a point while simultaneously allowing overshoots near a jump.

For a piecewise \(C^1\) periodic function, Dirichlet's convergence theorem gives
\[
 \lim_{N\to\infty}S_Nf(x)
 =\frac{f(x+0)+f(x-0)}2.
\]
This theorem is consistent with the sawtooth calculation; it does not assert uniform convergence in the presence of jumps.

In fact, the overshoot has an exact limiting profile. For the sawtooth, put \(x_N=\pi-t/N\) with fixed \(t>0\). The sine identity
\(\sin(n(\pi-y))=(-1)^{n+1}\sin(ny)\) yields
\[
 S_Nf(x_N)
 =2\sum_{n=1}^N\frac{\sin(nt/N)}n
 =\frac2N\sum_{n=1}^N
   \frac{\sin(tn/N)}{n/N}.
\]
This is a Riemann sum, so
\[
 \boxed{\lim_{N\to\infty}S_Nf(\pi-t/N)
 =2\int_0^t\frac{\sin u}{u}\,du.}
\]
At \(t=\pi\), the limit is approximately \(3.7039\), exceeding the left-hand value \(\pi\) by about \(0.5623\). Relative to the jump height \(2\pi\), the overshoot is about \(8.95\%\). Its spatial width shrinks like \(1/N\), but its height does not vanish: this is the Gibbs phenomenon.

\section{Fejér averaging repairs the kernel}

Instead of taking the last partial sum, average all the partial sums through \(N\):
\[
 \sigma_Nf=\frac1{N+1}\sum_{k=0}^NS_kf.
\]
Its convolution kernel is
\[
 \boxed{F_N(t)=\frac1{N+1}
 \left(\frac{\sin((N+1)t/2)}{\sin(t/2)}\right)^2.}
\]
Unlike \(D_N\), the Fejér kernel satisfies
\[
 F_N(t)\ge0,\qquad
 \frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}F_N(t)\,dt=1.
\]
Moreover, for any fixed \(\delta>0\),
\[
 \sup_{\delta\le|t|\le\pi}F_N(t)
 \le\frac{1}{(N+1)\sin^2(\delta/2)}.
\]
The mass of the kernel concentrates near \(t=0\).

For continuous periodic \(f\), let
\(\omega_f(\delta)=\sup_{|x-y|\le\delta}|f(x)-f(y)|\), with distance understood on the circle. Positivity allows us to estimate
\[
\begin{aligned}
 |\sigma_Nf(x)-f(x)|
 &\le \frac1{2\pi}
 \int_{-\pi}^{\pi}F_N(t)|f(x-t)-f(x)|\,dt\\
 &\le \omega_f(\delta)
  +\frac{2\|f\|_\infty}
         {(N+1)\sin^2(\delta/2)}.
\end{aligned}
\]
First choose \(\delta\) so the modulus of continuity is small, then choose \(N\) large. The bound is uniform in \(x\), proving
\[
 \boxed{\|\sigma_Nf-f\|_\infty\longrightarrow0.}
\]
Fejér's theorem is therefore more than a different summation convention: it changes a sign-changing convolution kernel into a positive approximate identity.

\section{Parseval and the precise meaning of completeness}

Trigonometric polynomials are uniformly dense in the continuous periodic functions, by Fejér's theorem. Continuous functions are dense in \(L^2(\mathbb T)\), so trigonometric polynomials are dense in \(L^2(\mathbb T)\). Together with orthogonality, this proves that \(e_n\), \(n\in\mathbb Z\), form an orthonormal basis.

Consequently the orthogonal projections satisfy \(S_Nf\to f\) in \(L^2\) for every \(f\in L^2(\mathbb T)\), and Parseval's identity is
\[
 \boxed{\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|f(x)|^2\,dx
 =\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}|\widehat f(n)|^2.}
\]
For the sawtooth, its complex coefficients satisfy
\[
 |\widehat f(n)|=\frac1{|n|}\quad(n\ne0).
\]
Therefore
\[
 \frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}x^2\,dx
 =\frac{\pi^2}{3}
 =2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{n^2}.
\]
This is a second, independent route to the Basel identity, now through orthogonal projection rather than evaluating a uniformly convergent cosine series.

Notice what Parseval does \emph{not} claim: the Fourier series need not reproduce the prescribed point value of \(f\) at a discontinuity. An \(L^2\) function is an equivalence class modulo changes on null sets; its Hilbert-space coordinates are insensitive to isolated point values.

\section{Regularity and the Sobolev threshold}

For a sufficiently regular periodic function, integration by parts gives
\[
 \widehat{f'}(n)=in\widehat f(n).
\]
Each derivative multiplies the \(n\)-th Fourier coefficient by \(in\). This motivates the Sobolev norm
\[
 \boxed{\|f\|_{H^s(\mathbb T)}^2
 =\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}(1+n^2)^s|\widehat f(n)|^2.}
\]
It is initially defined for trigonometric polynomials and extends to distributions for which the sum is finite.

For \(s>1/2\), Cauchy--Schwarz gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}|\widehat f(n)|
 &\le
 \left(\sum_n(1+n^2)^{-s}\right)^{1/2}
 \left(\sum_n(1+n^2)^s|\widehat f(n)|^2\right)^{1/2}\\
 &\le C_s\|f\|_{H^s}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The first sum is finite exactly when \(s>1/2\). Hence every \(H^s(\mathbb T)\) function with \(s>1/2\) has an absolutely and uniformly convergent Fourier series, determining a continuous representative. This proves the one-dimensional Sobolev embedding
\[
 H^s(\mathbb T)\hookrightarrow C^0(\mathbb T),
 \qquad s>\frac12.
\]
The threshold is not an arbitrary label: it comes directly from the summability of inverse frequency weights.

\section{Heat and waves act differently on the same coordinates}

Fourier characters diagonalize the Laplacian:
\[
 -\partial_x^2 e^{inx}=n^2e^{inx}.
\]
For initial data \(f\in L^2(\mathbb T)\), the periodic heat equation
\(u_t=\kappa u_{xx}\) has solution
\[
 \boxed{u(x,t)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}
  e^{-\kappa n^2t}\widehat f(n)e^{inx}},
 \qquad t>0.
\]
For every positive time, the Gaussian decay in \(n\) beats every polynomial growth factor \(n^m\). The heat solution is therefore smooth in \(x\), even if its initial data have jumps.

For the wave equation \(u_{tt}=c^2u_{xx}\) with initial displacement \(f\) and velocity \(g\), each nonzero Fourier mode solves a harmonic oscillator equation. The resulting formula is
\[
\begin{aligned}
 u(x,t)
 &=\bigl(\widehat f(0)+t\widehat g(0)\bigr)\\
 &\quad+\sum_{n\ne0}
 \left(\widehat f(n)\cos(c|n|t)
 +\frac{\widehat g(n)}{c|n|}\sin(c|n|t)\right)e^{inx}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The heat semigroup damps high frequencies; wave propagation rotates their phases without smoothing their amplitudes. The same orthogonal basis detects a fundamental difference between irreversible diffusion and reversible oscillation.

\section{Fourier series can represent distributions}

Point masses are not \(L^2\) functions, but their Fourier coefficients are meaningful by duality. For the periodic Dirac distribution at zero, define
\[
 \langle\delta_0,\varphi\rangle=\varphi(0)
\]
on smooth periodic test functions. Its Fourier coefficients in the integral normalization above are all \(1/(2\pi)\), so formally
\[
 \boxed{\delta_0=\frac1{2\pi}
   \sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}e^{inx}.}
\]
This equality is true as an equality of periodic distributions: pairing either side with a smooth test function gives \(\varphi(0)\). It is not a pointwise sum of ordinary functions.

The distributional viewpoint explains why frequency methods continue to work for singular initial data and fundamental solutions. Orthogonal projection controls mean-square approximation, positive kernels control uniform summation, Sobolev weights measure regularity, and weak Fourier series extend these constructions to objects more singular than functions.
