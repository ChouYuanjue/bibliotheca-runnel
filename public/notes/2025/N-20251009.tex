\notechapter{N-20251009}{From a Latent-Variable Model to a Trainable Generator}

A latent-variable model has a simple forward recipe: sample a hidden code \(z\), and then sample an observation \(x\) given that code. Even a mixture of simple conditional Gaussian laws can produce several peaks after the hidden cause is forgotten; determining which cause explains an observation then requires Bayes' rule. With images, the remaining questions are practical: what exactly does a neural decoder output, how do we measure an entire dataset under the model, and why do we need a recognition network when we can sample from the model already?\footnote{The model architecture and approximate-inference viewpoint follow Kingma and Welling, \emph{An Introduction to Variational Autoencoders} (2019), and C. Doersch, \href{https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.05908}{\emph{Tutorial on Variational Autoencoders}}.}

\section{A decoder produces probabilities, not just pixels}

For a latent code \(z\in\mathbb R^d\), the standard VAE starts with a simple prior such as
\[
 p(z)=\mathcal N(z;0,I).
\]
A neural decoder maps \(z\) to the parameters of a conditional distribution \(p_\theta(x\mid z)\). The model is
\[
 \boxed{p_\theta(x,z)=p(z)\,p_\theta(x\mid z).}
\]
The direction \(z\to x\) is the generative direction; unlike a reconstruction-only autoencoder, it can begin from a random latent draw without any observed image.

The observation distribution must match the data. If an image consists of binary pixels \(x_j\in\{0,1\}\), the decoder might output a number \(\pi_j(z)\in(0,1)\) for each pixel and define
\[
 p_\theta(x\mid z)
 =\prod_{j=1}^D
 \pi_j(z)^{x_j}(1-\pi_j(z))^{1-x_j}.
\]
Each output is the probability that a pixel is on, not the pixel itself. The negative log-likelihood becomes
\[
 -\log p_\theta(x\mid z)
 =-\sum_j[x_j\log\pi_j(z)+(1-x_j)\log(1-\pi_j(z))],
\]
which is binary cross-entropy.

For a real-valued feature vector, a Gaussian decoder might be preferable:
\[
 p_\theta(x\mid z)=
 \mathcal N(x;\mu_\theta(z),\sigma_x^2I).
\]
Up to terms independent of \(\theta\), the negative log-likelihood is
\[
 -\log p_\theta(x\mid z)
 =\frac{\|x-\mu_\theta(z)\|^2}{2\sigma_x^2}
   +\text{constant}.
\]
So the familiar squared reconstruction error is a probabilistic modeling choice, with the noise variance determining its scale. The likelihood specification matters as much as the neural-network architecture.

\section{Conditional Independence and Correlation}

The product over pixels assumes that the pixels are independent \emph{given \(z\)}. That is a computational simplification. It does not imply that pixels are independent in the generated dataset, because the same latent variable affects every pixel.

A tiny example makes the distinction sharp. Let \(Z\) be a fair bit. Given \(Z=z\), set \(X_1=z\) and \(X_2=z\) deterministically. The two observations are trivially independent conditional on \(Z\), but marginally
\[
 \Pr(X_1=1)=\Pr(X_2=1)=\frac12,\qquad
 \Pr(X_1=1,X_2=1)=\frac12
 \ne\frac14.
\]
Thus the shared latent cause has created dependence between the observed variables. This is why even a decoder using a factorized pixel likelihood can model correlated images after latent codes are marginalized out.

The price of a simple factorized decoder is also visible. Conditioned on a \emph{fixed} code \(z\), it cannot represent unexplained correlations between pixels beyond those already encoded by \(z\). A better latent representation or a richer likelihood may be needed when the data exhibit residual structure.

\section{A dataset means many hidden causes}

For observations \(x^{(1)},\ldots,x^{(N)}\), assume independent examples and give each example its own latent variable \(z^{(i)}\). The joint law is
\[
 p_\theta(x^{(1:N)},z^{(1:N)})
 =\prod_{i=1}^{N}
 p(z^{(i)})p_\theta(x^{(i)}\mid z^{(i)}).
\]
The same prior and decoder are used for every observation, while each observation has its own latent code. A small continuous example also reveals the geometry of the marginal distribution produced by a shared latent cause.

Learning by marginal maximum likelihood would maximize
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \log p_\theta(x^{(1:N)})
 &=\sum_{i=1}^N\log p_\theta(x^{(i)}),\\
 p_\theta(x)
 &=\int p(z)p_\theta(x\mid z)\,dz.
 \end{aligned}
\]
The integral is the obstacle. Even if the decoder evaluates quickly for one \(z\), integrating its output over all plausible latent codes can be very expensive.

\section{When Prior Sampling Fails}

The identity
\[
 p_\theta(x)=\mathbb E_{z\sim p(z)}[p_\theta(x\mid z)]
\]
suggests a Monte Carlo approximation by averaging \(p_\theta(x\mid z)\) over prior samples. This works in principle as an unbiased estimator of the likelihood, but it can be practically useless when the observation is explained by only a tiny part of latent space.

Here is an exact discrete example. Take \(K\) independent fair latent bits, \(Z=(Z_1,\ldots,Z_K)\). Let the observed bit \(X\) equal one exactly when every hidden bit equals one:
\[
 \Pr(X=1\mid Z=z)=
 \begin{cases}
 1,&z=(1,\ldots,1),\\
 0,&\text{otherwise}.
 \end{cases}
\]
Generating a sample is trivial. Yet
\[
 \boxed{\Pr(X=1)=2^{-K}.}
\]
If we draw \(M\) latent samples from the prior and average their likelihoods for the observed event \(X=1\), the estimate is exactly zero unless at least one sample hits the all-ones configuration. The probability of never hitting it is
\[
 \boxed{\left(1-2^{-K}\right)^M.}
\]
For \(K=20\), even ten thousand prior samples miss the relevant latent state with probability about \(0.9905\). To obtain a reasonable probability of observing even one relevant sample, \(M\) must be of order \(2^K\).

Once \(X=1\) is known, however, the posterior is concentrated on the all-ones code. The difficulty was not evaluating the decoder; it was sampling latent states without regard to the observed data. This is the basic reason approximate posterior inference is so valuable.

\section{Recognition is a fast approximate inverse}

The recognition network supplies
\[
 q_\phi(z\mid x)\approx p_\theta(z\mid x).
\]
For a continuous latent code, a common choice is a diagonal Gaussian,
\[
 q_\phi(z\mid x)
 =\mathcal N\bigl(z;
      \mu_\phi(x),\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_\phi^2(x))\bigr).
\]
The neural encoder computes \(\mu_\phi(x)\) and \(\sigma_\phi(x)\) from the input image, describing a whole distribution of plausible codes. The decoder and encoder answer different conditional questions:
\[
 \underbrace{p_\theta(x\mid z)}_{\text{what could this cause generate?}}
 \qquad\text{and}\qquad
 \underbrace{q_\phi(z\mid x)}_{\text{what might have caused this observation?}}.
\]

An ordinary variational method could optimize a separate approximate posterior for every training example. A VAE amortizes that computation: the same encoder parameters \(\phi\) map every new input to posterior parameters in a single forward pass. This makes large-dataset inference fast, but it constrains all approximations to come from one shared learned function.

There are now two distinct approximation errors to keep in mind. A diagonal Gaussian family may be unable to represent a multimodal posterior, and even within that family a learned encoder may not output the best variational parameters for every example. The evidence lower bound addresses the training objective and quantifies the discrepancy with the true posterior; the next mathematical step is to optimize that bound with gradients.
