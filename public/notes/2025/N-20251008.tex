\notechapter{N-20251008}{Latent Variable Models: Why Inference Is Harder Than Generation}

A generative model describes how unobserved causes produce observations. The forward direction can be simple even when the reverse problem of inferring causes from observations is difficult. This asymmetry is what motivates variational inference. The encoder--decoder shape is shared with ordinary autoencoders, but probability distributions play a different role in the two architectures. A two-component Gaussian mixture already reveals Bayes' rule, marginalization, an exact variational objective and the meaning of its approximation gap.\footnote{The latent-variable perspective follows Bishop, \emph{Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning}, Chapter 9, and Kingma and Welling, \href{https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.6114}{\emph{Auto-Encoding Variational Bayes}}.}


To picture the problem, imagine a drawing program trained on handwritten digits. A conventional autoencoder compresses an existing image and tries to reproduce it. A generative latent-variable model goes the other way: it first samples a hidden description \(z\), then uses a decoder to produce a new image. The decoder must represent \emph{uncertainty}; otherwise it merely outputs one deterministic reconstruction.

In a real image model, \(z\) might encode a digit's identity, stroke thickness, slant, and other hidden factors. These coordinates need not correspond to named human concepts, but they offer a compact set of variables from which the model can generate high-dimensional observations. The toy models below use one-dimensional \(x,z\), so every probability calculation remains visible.

\section{A hidden bit generates an observed real number}

Let \(Z\) take values \(-1,+1\) with equal probability, and fix \(\mu>0,\sigma>0\). Generate a real observation according to
\[
 \Pr(Z=+1)=\Pr(Z=-1)=\frac12,\qquad
 X\mid Z=z\sim\mathcal N(z\mu,\sigma^2).
\]
Equivalently,
\[
 X=\mu Z+\varepsilon,\qquad
 \varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2),
\]
with \(Z\) and \(\varepsilon\) independent. Sampling is trivial: flip a fair coin and add Gaussian noise to one of the two possible means.

Write
\[
 \varphi_\sigma(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}
                   e^{-t^2/(2\sigma^2)}.
\]
The joint density with respect to counting measure in \(z\) and Lebesgue measure in \(x\) is
\[
 p(x,z)=\frac12\varphi_\sigma(x-z\mu).
\]
To obtain the marginal density of the observation we must sum over the hidden possibilities:
\[
 \boxed{p(x)=\frac12\varphi_\sigma(x-\mu)
                +\frac12\varphi_\sigma(x+\mu).}
\]
This sum is a genuine probability density: integrating either Gaussian contributes \(1/2\). The value \(p(x)\) itself need not be at most one; the probabilities of events are integrals of \(p(x)\).


A plot is more revealing than the symbols alone. Take \(\mu=1.55\) and \(\sigma=0.8\). The two colored curves represent the possible observations given each hidden state. The black curve is the actual distribution after the hidden state has been forgotten.

The overlap is important. If we observe a value near the middle, a decoder tells us it \emph{could} have come from either component. To choose between those explanations requires an additional computation, the posterior.

The model can produce a distribution with two peaks. Symmetry gives \(p'(0)=0\). Using
\(\varphi_\sigma''(t)=(t^2-\sigma^2)\varphi_\sigma(t)/\sigma^4\), we obtain
\[
 p''(0)=\frac{\mu^2-\sigma^2}{\sigma^4}\,p(0).
\]
If \(\mu>\sigma\), the origin is a strict local minimum. Since \(p(x)\to0\) as \(|x|\to\infty\), there must be a peak on each side of the origin. A mixture of individually unimodal Gaussians can therefore be bimodal.

\section{Bayes' rule is a reverse generative computation}

After observing \(X=x\), the two possible latent explanations have posterior weights
\[
 \Pr(Z=z\mid X=x)=\frac{p(x,z)}{p(x)}.
\]
The likelihood ratio cancels the common Gaussian normalizer:
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \log\frac{\Pr(Z=+1\mid x)}{\Pr(Z=-1\mid x)}
 &=\log\frac{\varphi_\sigma(x-\mu)}
                {\varphi_\sigma(x+\mu)}\\
 &=\frac{(x+\mu)^2-(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}
 =\frac{2\mu x}{\sigma^2}.
 \end{aligned}
\]
Therefore, writing \(\operatorname{sigmoid}(t)=1/(1+e^{-t})\),
\[
 \boxed{\Pr(Z=+1\mid x)
       =\operatorname{sigmoid}\left(\frac{2\mu x}{\sigma^2}\right).}
\]
The posterior is most uncertain at \(x=0\); for large positive \(x\), the explanation \(Z=+1\) becomes strongly favored. At the same time, a positive observation is never \emph{proof} that the positive latent state occurred: both Gaussian likelihoods have nonzero tails.


For example, at \(\mu=\sigma=1\),
\[
 \begin{array}{c|ccc}
 \text{observation }x&-1&0&+1\\ \hline
 \Pr(Z=+1\mid x)&0.119&0.500&0.881
 \end{array}
\]
to three decimal places. This is a \emph{soft assignment}. It reports how much of the posterior probability belongs to each possible cause, rather than declaring one cause with certainty.

The general latent-variable model replaces this two-term sum by
\[
 p_\theta(x)=\int p_\theta(x\mid z)p(z)\,dz,\qquad
 p_\theta(z\mid x)=\frac{p_\theta(x\mid z)p(z)}{p_\theta(x)}.
\]
Generation uses \(p(z)\) followed by \(p_\theta(x\mid z)\). Inference needs the normalizing integral \(p_\theta(x)\), which can become intractable when \(z\) is high-dimensional or the decoder is nonlinear.

\section{A soluble continuous model shows what is lost}

There is one continuous example in which the inverse computation is still exact. Let
\[
 Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1),\qquad
 X\mid Z=z\sim\mathcal N(z,\sigma^2).
\]
Completing the square in the joint density gives
\[
 -\frac12z^2-\frac{(x-z)^2}{2\sigma^2}
 =-\frac{1+\sigma^{-2}}2
   \left(z-\frac{x}{1+\sigma^2}\right)^2
   -\frac{x^2}{2(1+\sigma^2)}.
\]
Consequently,
\[
 \boxed{Z\mid X=x\sim
 \mathcal N\left(\frac{x}{1+\sigma^2},
                \frac{\sigma^2}{1+\sigma^2}\right),}
\]
and
\[
 X\sim\mathcal N(0,1+\sigma^2).
\]
The posterior mean is a shrinkage estimator: a noisy observation is pulled toward the prior mean zero. Its coefficient \(1/(1+\sigma^2)\) decreases as the observation noise grows.

Now change only the decoder to
\[
 X=Z^2+\varepsilon,\qquad
 \varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2).
\]

Here is the geometric reason the nonlinear case is harder. The decoder \(z\mapsto z^2\) \emph{folds} the latent line: two distant causes can produce the same observation.

For \(x>0\) and small noise, both \(z\approx+\sqrt x\) and \(z\approx-\sqrt x\) are plausible explanations, so the posterior is generally bimodal rather than Gaussian. Sampling from the generative model remains easy, but completing a single quadratic square no longer solves the inverse problem. A nonlinear neural decoder greatly increases this gap between forward sampling and posterior computation.

\section{An Exactly Solvable Variational Example}

Return to the hidden-bit mixture. Suppose we approximate the true posterior with an arbitrary Bernoulli distribution
\[
 q_r(+1)=r,\qquad q_r(-1)=1-r,\qquad 0<r<1.
\]
Define
\[
 \mathcal L(r;x)=
 \sum_{z\in\{-1,+1\}}q_r(z)\log p(x,z)
 -\sum_{z\in\{-1,+1\}}q_r(z)\log q_r(z).
\]
Writing \(p_\pm=p(x,\pm1)\), we have
\[
 \mathcal L(r;x)
 =r\log p_+ +(1-r)\log p_-
   -r\log r-(1-r)\log(1-r).
\]
Differentiation gives
\[
 \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial r}
 =\log\frac{p_+}{p_-}-\log\frac r{1-r},\qquad
 \frac{\partial^2\mathcal L}{\partial r^2}
 =-\frac1{r(1-r)}<0.
\]
The objective is strictly concave. Its unique maximizer satisfies
\[
 \frac{r_*}{1-r_*}=\frac{p_+}{p_-}
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 \boxed{r_*=\Pr(Z=+1\mid X=x).}
\]
Thus optimizing this objective recovers Bayes' posterior exactly in a problem small enough to solve on paper.

The value at the optimum is the marginal log-likelihood:
\[
 \mathcal L(r_*;x)=\log(p_++p_-)=\log p(x).
\]
For an arbitrary \(r\), direct subtraction gives the stronger identity
\[
 \boxed{\log p(x)-\mathcal L(r;x)
 =D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(q_r\,\|\,p(\cdot\mid x)\bigr)\ge0.}
\]
Here \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p)=\sum_zq(z)\log(q(z)/p(z))\). The inequality follows from convexity of \(t\log t\), or from the log-sum inequality; equality holds if and only if the approximate posterior equals the true one.

This finite example is the Gibbs variational principle in concrete form. It explains both the lower bound and its exact gap before we make either distribution an output of a neural network.

\section{Why a neural encoder is useful}

For a large nonlinear generative model, solving the optimal posterior for every \(x\) separately would be expensive. A recognition model \(q_\phi(z\mid x)\) instead learns a \emph{function of the observation} that produces an approximate posterior. This is amortized inference: computational effort is shared across many data points.

A continuous VAE often chooses a diagonal Gaussian family
\[
 q_\phi(z\mid x)
 =\mathcal N\bigl(z;\mu_\phi(x),
           \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_\phi^2(x))\bigr).
\]
The parameters \(\mu_\phi(x)\) and \(\sigma_\phi(x)\) are functions computed by an encoder. A diagonal Gaussian is convenient to sample and evaluate, but it cannot faithfully represent the two separated modes of a strongly multimodal posterior. The restriction of the variational family introduces a real approximation error, distinct from optimization error.


The encoder is useful because it learns to perform the approximate inverse operation \emph{quickly for each new observation}. It usually does not output a single latent coordinate. For a diagonal-Gaussian VAE it outputs a mean and a vector of standard deviations, describing a region of plausible latent codes.

At generation time, the input image and inference network are not needed. Draw \(z\sim p(z)\) from the prior and then \(x\sim p_\theta(x\mid z)\) from the decoder. At inference time, start with an observed \(x\) and use \(q_\phi(z\mid x)\) to approximate where it may have come from. During training, both paths cooperate through a likelihood lower bound.

The exact mixture and Gaussian calculations establish the core issue. A generative story determines a joint law, marginalization measures how likely an observation is, and Bayes' rule inverts the story. When that inversion is hard, a tractable family \(q_\phi(z\mid x)\) replaces it. The next mathematical task is to optimize the resulting lower bound and differentiate it through random sampling.
