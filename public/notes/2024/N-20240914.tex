\notechapter{N-20240914}{Divisors on Curves: Zeros, Poles, and Riemann--Roch}

On a compact connected Riemann surface \(X\), a nonzero meromorphic function \(f\) has an isolated integer order at every point. In a local coordinate \(z(p)=0\),
\[
 f(z)=z^m u(z),\qquad u(0)\ne0,\qquad \operatorname{ord}_p(f)=m.
\]
A change of coordinate multiplies \(z\) by a holomorphic unit, so \(m\) is intrinsic. The basic treatment here follows Forster, \emph{Lectures on Riemann Surfaces}, and the definitions and degree results of the Stacks Project.\footnote{\href{https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/02AQ}{Stacks Project, Tag 02AQ}, with related material in Tag 02RS.}

\section{A divisor remembers every local condition}

A divisor is a finite integral sum \(D=\sum_p n_p[p]\). Its degree is \(\deg D=\sum_p n_p\). The principal divisor of a nonzero meromorphic function is
\[
 (f)=\sum_p\operatorname{ord}_p(f)[p],\qquad
 (fg)=(f)+(g).
\]
On the Riemann sphere \(\mathbb P^1\), the coordinate \(w=1/z\) near infinity gives
\[
 z-a=w^{-1}(1-aw),\qquad
 (z-a)=[a]-[\infty].
\]
In particular, the affine zero at \(a\) must be balanced by a pole at infinity.

\begin{theorem}[Degree zero]
For a nonzero meromorphic function on a compact connected Riemann surface, \(\deg(f)=0\).
\end{theorem}
For a nonconstant \(f\), the induced holomorphic map \(X\to\mathbb P^1\) has the same degree when counted above \(0\) or above \(\infty\). The respective local multiplicities are exactly the orders of its zeros and poles. For constant \(f\ne0\), the divisor is zero.

\section{Allowed poles form a vector space}

For divisors write \(D\ge0\) when each coefficient is nonnegative. Set
\[
 L(D)=\{0\}\cup\{f\ne0\text{ meromorphic}:(f)+D\ge0\},
 \qquad\ell(D)=\dim_{\mathbb C}L(D).
\]
With \(D=n[\infty]\) on \(\mathbb P^1\), the condition forbids finite poles and permits a pole of order at most \(n\) at infinity. Such rational functions are exactly polynomials of degree at most \(n\):
\[
 L(n[\infty])=\operatorname{span}\{1,z,\ldots,z^n\},
 \qquad\ell(n[\infty])=n+1\quad(n\ge0).
\]
Prescribing a zero at \(0\) changes the divisor to \(2[\infty]-[0]\), and gives
\[
 L(2[\infty]-[0])=\operatorname{span}\{z,z^2\}.
\]
Adding a further zero at \(1\) leaves just \(\mathbb C z(z-1)\). This makes the bookkeeping precise: a pole allowance raises the possible degree, while a vanishing condition imposes a linear constraint.

\begin{proposition}
If \(\deg D<0\), then \(L(D)=\{0\}\).
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
If \(f\ne0\) belonged to \(L(D)\), the effective divisor \((f)+D\) would have nonnegative degree, whereas
\(\deg((f)+D)=\deg(f)+\deg D=\deg D<0\), a contradiction.
\end{proof}

\section{Line bundles explain the local-to-global problem}

On a small open set \(U\), choose a meromorphic function \(g_U\) with orders prescribed by \(D|_U\). The allowable local functions are \(g_U^{-1}\mathcal O_X(U)\). On overlapping charts \(U,V\), the ratio \(g_U/g_V\) is holomorphic and nowhere zero. Those ratios are precisely transition functions for a holomorphic line bundle \(\mathcal O(D)\). Thus
\[
 L(D)=H^0(X,\mathcal O(D)).
\]
The vector space is finite-dimensional for compact \(X\). Divisors turn local restrictions on poles into globally compatible sections; the obstruction is that independently chosen local sections need not glue.

\section{Canonical divisors and the genus}

A meromorphic differential is locally \(h(z)\,dz\). Changing to another local coordinate multiplies the coefficient by a nonzero holomorphic factor, so \(\operatorname{ord}_p(h(z)\,dz)\) is well defined. The divisor \(K=(\omega)\) of a nonzero meromorphic differential is a canonical divisor. Different choices of \(\omega\) differ by a meromorphic function, hence determine linearly equivalent divisors.

On \(\mathbb P^1\) the calculation
\[
 dz=-w^{-2}\,dw,\qquad w=1/z,
\]
shows \(K=-2[\infty]\) and \(\deg K=-2\). A complex torus \(\mathbb C/\Lambda\), by contrast, carries the nowhere-vanishing holomorphic differential \(dz\), so \(K=0\). The general degree formula is
\[
 \deg K=2g-2
\]
for a compact Riemann surface of genus \(g\). This is a global theorem; the sphere and torus are tests of its normalization, not its proof.

\section{Riemann--Roch counts sections and obstructions}

\begin{theorem}[Riemann--Roch]
If \(D\) is a divisor on a compact connected Riemann surface of genus \(g\), then
\[
 \ell(D)-\ell(K-D)=\deg D+1-g.
\]
\end{theorem}
We quote the general theorem rather than disguise its cohomological proof as a calculation. The correction \(\ell(K-D)\) counts meromorphic differentials whose divisor is at least \(D\); these are the global constraints missing from a naive dimension count.

Take \(X=\mathbb P^1\), \(D=n[\infty]\) with \(n\ge0\), and \(K=-2[\infty]\). Since \(\deg(K-D)=-2-n<0\), the preceding proposition gives \(\ell(K-D)=0\). Hence
\[
 \ell(D)=n+1,
\]
recovering the explicit polynomial basis. The theorem has passed a nontrivial consistency check.

On an elliptic curve \(g=1\), \(K\sim0\). For a divisor of positive degree \(d\), \(\deg(-D)<0\) gives \(\ell(-D)=0\), and so \(\ell(D)=d\). In particular
\[
 \ell([p])=1,\qquad \ell(2[p])=2.
\]
The first equality says that a nonconstant meromorphic function cannot have just one simple pole. The second says a function with at most one double pole does exist. This is the first dimension-count explanation for why elliptic functions naturally develop double poles.

\section{Ramification is another kind of local multiplicity}

For a nonconstant holomorphic map \(F:X\to Y\) of degree \(d\), a local coordinate expression at \(p\) has the form \(z\mapsto z^{e_p}\) after suitable changes of coordinates. The Riemann--Hurwitz theorem states
\[
 2g_X-2=d(2g_Y-2)+\sum_{p\in X}(e_p-1).
\]
For the rational map \(F(z)=z^d\) on \(\mathbb P^1\), only \(0\) and \(\infty\) ramify, each with index \(d\). The right-hand side is
\[
 -2d+(d-1)+(d-1)=-2,
\]
as required for a sphere.

Riemann--Roch and Riemann--Hurwitz answer different questions. One counts sections subject to allowed zeros and poles; the other compares the canonical divisors of curves connected by a map. Their common arithmetic is the addition of local integer orders to obtain a global constraint.
