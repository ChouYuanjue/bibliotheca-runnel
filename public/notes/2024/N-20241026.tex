\section{Which geometry can a polynomial equation remember?}

Consider the two equations \(x=0\) and \(x^2=0\) over a field \(k\).
They have the same solutions in \(k\), but the quotient algebras
\[
 k[x]/(x)\cong k,\qquad k[x]/(x^2)
\]
are different: the class \(\epsilon\) of \(x\) in the second ring is nonzero with \(\epsilon^2=0\).  Before defining any topology, we have encountered a choice. Do we study \emph{ordinary point sets}, where these equations agree, or an algebraic space retaining infinitesimal equations, where they differ? This note follows the standard construction of affine geometry from rings, as organized in the \emph{Stacks Project}, especially the chapters on spectra and affine schemes.\footnote{\href{https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/00DY}{Stacks Project, Tag 00DY}, and Tag 01HR on affine schemes.}

First let \(k\) be algebraically closed, and put \(A=k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]\).  For an ideal \(I\subseteq A\), define
\[
 V(I)=\{a\in k^n:f(a)=0\text{ for all }f\in I\}.
\]
Conversely, a subset \(X\subseteq k^n\) determines
\[
 I(X)=\{f\in A:f(a)=0\text{ for every }a\in X\}.
\]
The operations reverse containment: if \(I\subseteq J\) then \(V(J)\subseteq V(I)\).  They also exchange natural operations:
\[
 V(I+J)=V(I)\cap V(J),\qquad
 V(IJ)=V(I)\cup V(J).
\]
For the two axes, \(V(x)\cup V(y)=V(xy)\), whereas
\(V(x)\cap V(y)=V(x,y)=\{(0,0)\}\).

\section{What Points Forget in Coordinate Rings}

The coordinate ring of an algebraic set \(X\) is
\[
 k[X]=A/I(X).
\]
Its elements are polynomial functions restricted to \(X\); two polynomials define the same function on \(X\) precisely when their difference belongs to \(I(X)\).

Hilbert's Nullstellensatz is the crucial bridge. For an ideal \(I\) over an algebraically closed field,
\[
 \boxed{I(V(I))=\sqrt I
       =\{f\in A:\text{some }f^m\in I\}.}
\]
Thus point sets detect radical ideals. For instance, \(I=(x^2)\) and \(\sqrt I=(x)\) yield identical classical zero sets, and the associated coordinate ring of the \emph{set} is \(k[x]/(x)\). The thicker quotient \(k[x]/(x^2)\) retains information that classical points have forgotten.

\begin{example}[The curved hyperbola is an affine punctured line]
The parabola \(Y=V(y-x^2)\) has
\[
 k[Y]=k[x,y]/(y-x^2)\cong k[x]
\]
by eliminating \(y\). The hyperbola \(H=V(xy-1)\) has
\[
 k[H]=k[x,y]/(xy-1)\cong k[x,x^{-1}].
\]
The projection \((x,y)\mapsto x\) identifies \(H\) with \(\mathbb A^1\setminus\{0\}\); its inverse is \(t\mapsto(t,t^{-1})\). The identity can be checked in either direction and illustrates why the algebra of functions can reveal structure obscured by a drawing.
\end{example}

\section{Closed sets and the deliberately coarse topology}

The closed subsets of affine space are the sets \(V(I)\). They form a topology because finite unions and arbitrary intersections of algebraic sets are again algebraic. Over an infinite field, a nonzero polynomial in one variable has only finitely many roots, so a proper Zariski-closed subset of \(\mathbb A^1\) is finite. Consequently nonempty Zariski-open subsets of the affine line have finite complements. This topology is useful for algebraic dependence; it does not try to measure Euclidean distance.

For \(f\in k[X]\), the distinguished open set
\[
 D(f)=\{p\in X:f(p)\ne0\}
\]
is the region where division by \(f\) makes sense.  In particular,
\[
 D(x)\subseteq\mathbb A^1,\qquad
 \mathcal O(D(x))=k[x,x^{-1}].
\]
This can be derived directly: localizing \(k[x]\) at the multiplicative set \(\{1,x,x^2,\ldots\}\) introduces fractions \(g(x)/x^m\). Algebraic geometry uses these localizations in place of functions defined using arbitrary analytic neighborhoods.

\section{Sheaves, Stalks, and Gluing}

To see how local data are organized, it is convenient to pass to the full spectrum. For any commutative ring \(R\) with \(1\),
\[
 \operatorname{Spec}R=\{\mathfrak p:\mathfrak p\text{ a prime ideal of }R\},
\]
with closed sets \(V(I)=\{\mathfrak p:I\subseteq\mathfrak p\}\) and basic opens \(D(f)=\{\mathfrak p:f\notin\mathfrak p\}\).
The \emph{structure sheaf} on this space has the decisive local formulas
\[
 \boxed{\mathcal O_{\operatorname{Spec}R}(D(f))=R_f,\qquad
 \mathcal O_{\operatorname{Spec}R,\mathfrak p}=R_{\mathfrak p}.}
\]
The second is the stalk at \(\mathfrak p\): denominators are allowed exactly when they lie outside \(\mathfrak p\).

Why is a sheaf necessary? If \(D(f)=\bigcup_iD(f_i)\), a function described by sections \(s_i\in R_{f_i}\) must satisfy
\[
 s_i|_{D(f_if_j)}=s_j|_{D(f_if_j)}
\]
on every overlap. The sheaf condition asserts that compatible descriptions glue uniquely. For a finite standard-open cover of \(\operatorname{Spec}R\), the algebraic content is the exact sequence
\[
 0\longrightarrow R\longrightarrow\bigoplus_i R_{f_i}
 \longrightarrow\bigoplus_{i,j}R_{f_if_j},
\]
where the last arrow sends \((s_i)\) to the differences of its restrictions. This is not merely a slogan about gluing: it says exactly what agreement on overlaps means and why a global section is determined by its local pieces.

As a small check, \(D(t)\cup D(1-t)=\operatorname{Spec}k[t]\), since the ideal \((t,1-t)\) contains \(1\). The two representations \(t/t\in k[t,t^{-1}]\) and \((1-t)/(1-t)\in k[t,(1-t)^{-1}]\) agree on the overlap and glue to the global function \(1\).

\section{Branches and Infinitesimal Directions}

Consider again
\[
 X=V(xy)\subseteq\mathbb A^2,\qquad R=k[x,y]/(xy).
\]
Its two irreducible components correspond to the minimal primes \((x)\) and \((y)\). The open \(D(x)\) has ring
\[
 R_x\cong k[x,x^{-1}],
\]
because \(xy=0\) and \(x\) has become invertible, forcing \(y=0\). Similarly \(R_y\cong k[y,y^{-1}]\). On these opens, only one branch remains. Their intersection \(D(xy)\) is empty.

The origin, however, lies in neither \(D(x)\) nor \(D(y)\). Its local ring is
\[
 R_{(x,y)}=\bigl(k[x,y]/(xy)\bigr)_{(x,y)}.
\]
The maximal ideal \(\mathfrak m=(x,y)R_{(x,y)}\) satisfies
\[
 \dim_k\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2=2,
\]
since the linear classes of \(x\) and \(y\) survive independently; the relation \(xy=0\) only appears in degree two. A smooth curve has one-dimensional tangent space at a smooth closed point. Thus the local ring detects the crossing as singular, even though each punctured branch individually looks like a line.

The nilpotent example \(k[\epsilon]/(\epsilon^2)\) makes a different point. Its spectrum has a single prime \((\epsilon)\), hence one underlying topological point, but its global sections have the form
\[
 a+b\epsilon,\qquad \epsilon^2=0.
\]
Topology alone cannot distinguish it from \(\operatorname{Spec}k\). The structure sheaf does. This is why the geometry of schemes retains both prime ideals as points and rings of local functions.

\section{Morphisms reverse arrows on rings}

For affine schemes, a ring homomorphism \(\varphi:B\to A\) induces a map of spectra
\[
 \operatorname{Spec}A\longrightarrow\operatorname{Spec}B,
 \qquad\mathfrak p\longmapsto\varphi^{-1}(\mathfrak p).
\]
The inverse image of a prime ideal is prime, and the preimage of \(D(b)\) is \(D(\varphi(b))\); hence this map is continuous and compatible with local sections. Conversely, an affine-scheme morphism induces such a homomorphism on global functions. Schematically,
\[
 X\longrightarrow Y\quad\leadsto\quad
 \Gamma(Y,\mathcal O_Y)\longrightarrow\Gamma(X,\mathcal O_X).
\]

One can see this without any categorical machinery. The map
\[
 \mathbb A^1\longrightarrow\mathbb A^2,\qquad t\longmapsto(t,t^2)
\]
pulls the coordinate functions \(x,y\) back to \(t,t^2\); the induced ring map is
\[
 k[x,y]\longrightarrow k[t],\qquad x\mapsto t,\quad y\mapsto t^2,
\]
whose kernel is \((y-x^2)\). This single calculation connects the geometry of the parabola, its coordinate ring, and the reversal of arrows. Localizations and stalks make the same correspondence meaningful near every point.
