\notechapter{N-20220913}{Divisibility as Arithmetic in Quotient Rings}

The most productive step in many divisibility problems is to stop treating the divisor as a large integer to be compared with an even larger expression. Once the divisor \(m\) is fixed, calculations take place among residue classes in \(\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z\). The familiar operations of greatest common divisors, Bezout coefficients, and the Chinese remainder theorem describe the structure of that ring.\footnote{See the introductory number theory and congruence lectures of MIT 18.781: \url{https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-781-theory-of-numbers-spring-2012/pages/lecture-notes/}.}

\section{A necessary condition can collapse the search}

Suppose a prime has the form
\[
 p=x(x+1)-4,\qquad x\in\mathbb Z_{>0}.
\]
The product of consecutive integers is even, so \(p\) is even. The only even positive prime is \(2\). Therefore \(x(x+1)=6\) and \(x=2\), which indeed gives \(p=2\). The short argument has two parts: parity narrows the candidates, and substitution verifies the surviving candidate.

A similar reduction works even when the divisor depends on the unknown integer. Consider
\[
 x^2+1\mid x^3-x,\qquad x\in\mathbb Z_{>0}.
\]
Since \(x^2\equiv-1\pmod{x^2+1}\), the condition is equivalent to
\[
 x^2+1\mid2x.
\]
For \(x>1\), \(x^2+1>2x>0\), making divisibility impossible. At \(x=1\), the dividend vanishes, so \(x=1\) is the unique positive solution.

\section{Ideals explain why remainders preserve information}

For nonzero \(m\in\mathbb Z\), the congruence \(a\equiv b\pmod m\) means \(a-b\in m\mathbb Z\). In the quotient ring \(\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z\), that is simply equality of the classes \([a]=[b]\). Polynomial or exponential expressions respect this equality, so we can simplify them before asking whether the original expression is divisible by \(m\).

The ideal \((a)=a\mathbb Z\) contains the multiples of \(a\), and
\[
 a\mid b\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad (b)\subseteq(a).
\]
The reversal of inclusion is useful. Bezout's identity implies
\[
 (a)+(b)=(\gcd(a,b)),\qquad
 (a)\cap(b)=(\operatorname{lcm}(a,b))
\]
for nonzero integers, using positive gcd and lcm. Thus gcd and lcm describe two universal ways to combine divisibility conditions.

A unit in \(\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z\) is a class \([a]\) possessing a multiplicative inverse. Such an inverse exists exactly when \(\gcd(a,m)=1\): the Bezout equation \(au+mv=1\) becomes \([a][u]=[1]\). By contrast, modulo \(6\), the nonzero classes \([2]\) and \([3]\) multiply to zero. This zero-divisor phenomenon explains why cancellation by an arbitrary nonzero class is invalid.

\section{A polynomial remainder hidden in an integer problem}

For integer \(x\), consider
\[
 x^2+x+1\mid x^4-2.
\]
The identity
\[
 x^3-1=(x-1)(x^2+x+1)
\]
shows that \(x^3\equiv1\pmod{x^2+x+1}\), whence
\[
 x^4-2\equiv x-2\pmod{x^2+x+1}.
\]
For positive \(x\), the divisor exceeds \(|x-2|\) except when \(x=2\), and the small remaining value \(x=1\) also fails. At \(x=2\), \(x^4-2=14\) and \(x^2+x+1=7\), so \(x=2\) is the unique positive solution.

There is a parallel computation in the polynomial quotient \(k[t]/(t^2+t+1)\), where the symbol \(t\) satisfies \(t^3=1\). That statement is about a class of a polynomial, independent of any integer substituted for \(t\). The integer problem evaluates the same polynomial identity at a particular \(x\), then interprets it as a congruence. Keeping these two uses of a symbol separate prevents a common logical slip.

\section{The Chinese remainder theorem builds solutions}

Suppose \(m,n\) are coprime. Choose \(u,v\in\mathbb Z\) satisfying \(um+vn=1\). Then the classes
\[
 e_m=vn,\qquad e_n=um
\]
satisfy
\[
 e_m\equiv1\pmod m,\quad e_m\equiv0\pmod n,\qquad
 e_n\equiv0\pmod m,\quad e_n\equiv1\pmod n.
\]
Therefore the simultaneous system
\[
 x\equiv a\pmod m,\qquad x\equiv b\pmod n
\]
has solution
\[
 \boxed{x\equiv ae_m+be_n\pmod{mn}.}
\]
Any two solutions differ by a multiple of both \(m\) and \(n\), hence by a multiple of \(mn\); the solution is unique modulo \(mn\).

For example, solve \(x\equiv2\pmod3\) and \(x\equiv4\pmod5\). The selectors \(10\equiv1\pmod3,\,0\pmod5\) and \(6\equiv0\pmod3,\,1\pmod5\) give
\[
 x\equiv2\cdot10+4\cdot6=44\equiv14\pmod{15}.
\]
These selectors are idempotents: modulo \(15\), \(10^2\equiv10\), \(6^2\equiv6\), \(10\cdot6\equiv0\), and \(10+6\equiv1\). Their algebraic behavior is exactly that of coordinate projections in the product ring
\[
 \mathbb Z/15\mathbb Z\cong\mathbb Z/3\mathbb Z\times\mathbb Z/5\mathbb Z.
\]
The general theorem for pairwise coprime moduli follows by the same Bezout construction. It is an explicit decomposition, rather than a mysterious claim that local solutions can be assembled globally.

\section{Odd powers and the arithmetic of a reflection}

A large example is
\[
 S=1^{1983}+2^{1983}+\cdots+1983^{1983}.
\]
The claim is that \(S\) is divisible by the triangular number
\[
 T=\frac{1983\cdot1984}{2}=1983\cdot992.
\]
The parity of the number of terms matters. Since \(1983\) is odd, pairing \(j\) with \(1983-j\) for \(1\le j\le1982\) gives \(1983\mid S\). Pairing instead \(j\) with \(1984-j\) gives
\[
 j^{1983}+(1984-j)^{1983}\equiv0\pmod{992},
\]
since \(1984-j\equiv-j\pmod{992}\). The middle term \(j=992\), which is its own partner, also vanishes modulo \(992\). Hence \(992\mid S\). Because \(1983\) and \(992\) are coprime, \(T\mid S\).

This method is algebra in a quotient ring together with a reflection symmetry of residue classes. The same strategy generalizes: instead of expanding a sum with thousands of terms, search for a transformation of the index that makes contributions cancel after passing to a suitable modulus.
