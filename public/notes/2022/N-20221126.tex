\section{Congruence is equality in a quotient ring}

For \(n\ge2\), the relation
\[
 a\equiv b\pmod n\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad n\mid(a-b)
\]
partitions \(\mathbb Z\) into \(n\) classes. It is an equivalence relation and respects the two ring operations:
\[
 a\equiv b,\quad c\equiv d\pmod n
 \ \Longrightarrow\ a+c\equiv b+d,\quad ac\equiv bd\pmod n.
\]
The residue class \([a]\) is the coset \(a+n\mathbb Z\), and the quotient
\[
 \mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z
\]
is a ring. A complete residue system consists of one representative from each of its classes. For example, \(0,1,\ldots,n-1\) is one such system, but replacing any representative by a congruent integer leaves it complete.

The purpose of reducing a complicated expression is therefore algebraic: evaluating a polynomial of integers and then passing to the quotient gives the same answer as reducing every integer first. Modular arithmetic is the image of integer arithmetic under a ring homomorphism, which explains why these reductions compose reliably. For the number-theoretic organization we follow Abhinav Kumar's MIT 18.781 course notes on congruences, Euler's theorem, the Chinese remainder theorem, and primitive roots.\footnote{\url{https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-781-theory-of-numbers-spring-2012/pages/lecture-notes/}}

\begin{example}[Three ways to avoid multiplying large numbers]
The last digit of \(15^{200}\) is \(5\), because \(15^r\equiv5\pmod{10}\) for every positive integer \(r\). Modulo \(17\),
\[
 15^{100}\equiv(-2)^{100}=2^{100},\qquad
 2^4\equiv-1,\quad 2^8\equiv1,
\]
so \(2^{100}\equiv(2^8)^{12}2^4\equiv16\pmod{17}\). Finally,
\[
\begin{aligned}
 &2001\cdot2002+2003\cdot2004+2005\cdot2006\\
 &\qquad\equiv21\cdot22+23\cdot24+25\cdot26\\
 &\qquad\equiv12+12+20\equiv44\pmod{45}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The examples illustrate reduction of a constant base, reduction by a multiplicative period, and reduction before distributing products.
\end{example}

Quadratic residues provide another useful finite check. Since every integer is congruent to \(0,\ldots,7\pmod8\), squaring gives
\[
 \{x^2\bmod8:x\in\mathbb Z\}=\{0,1,4\}.
\]
The eight possibilities can be reduced further: an odd integer has square \(1\pmod8\); an even integer is either divisible by \(4\), giving square \(0\pmod8\), or is \(2\pmod4\), giving square \(4\pmod8\).

\section{Reduced residue systems form the group of units}

A class \([a]\in\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z\) is invertible if and only if \(\gcd(a,n)=1\). Indeed, Bézout's identity \(ua+vn=1\) supplies an inverse of \([a]\), whereas an inverse modulo \(n\) implies \(\gcd(a,n)=1\).

Consequently the reduced residue classes form the multiplicative group
\[
 (\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z)^\times.
\]
Its cardinality is Euler's totient
\[
 \varphi(n)=\#(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z)^\times.
\]
For \(n=p^r\), precisely the multiples of \(p\) are excluded, so
\[
 \varphi(p^r)=p^r-p^{r-1}.
\]
For coprime \(m,n\), the Chinese remainder theorem gives a ring isomorphism
\[
 \mathbb Z/mn\mathbb Z\simeq
 \mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z\times\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z,
\]
which restricts to the unit groups. Their sizes multiply:
\[
 \varphi(mn)=\varphi(m)\varphi(n).
\]
Factoring \(n=\prod_{p\mid n}p^{v_p(n)}\) now derives
\[
 \boxed{\varphi(n)=n\prod_{p\mid n}(1-p^{-1}).}
\]
This is a deduction from counting units and the CRT, not an unrelated formula to memorize.

\section{Exponent reduction is a statement about element order}

For a unit \(a\pmod n\), define its multiplicative order by
\[
 \operatorname{ord}_n(a)=\min\{d\ge1:a^d\equiv1\pmod n\}.
\]
The finite-group theorem of Lagrange implies
\[
 \operatorname{ord}_n(a)\mid\varphi(n),\qquad
 a^{\varphi(n)}\equiv1\pmod n.
\]
This is Euler's theorem; it requires \(\gcd(a,n)=1\). A much smaller order may exist. For example,
\[
 2^4\equiv-1\pmod{17},\quad 2^8\equiv1\pmod{17},
\]
so \(\operatorname{ord}_{17}(2)=8\), whereas \(\varphi(17)=16\). The exponent \(100\) can be reduced modulo \(8\), the actual group order of the cyclic subgroup generated by \(2\). By contrast, \(15\pmod{10}\) is not a unit, so applying Euler's theorem to the last-digit problem would be invalid.

Consider the sequence
\[
 F_k=12^{2k+1}+36^k+5^k+3,\qquad k\ge1.
\]
Every term has \(F_k\equiv0\pmod4\). A finer residue computation gives
\[
 F_1\equiv12\pmod{16},\qquad
 F_k\equiv5^k+3\pmod{16}\quad(k\ge2),
\]
because \(12\cdot144^k\) and \(36^k\) are divisible by \(16\) for \(k\ge2\). The powers \(5^k\pmod{16}\) cycle with period four:
\[
 5,\quad9,\quad13,\quad1.
\]
It follows that
\[
 \boxed{16\mid F_k\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad k\equiv3\pmod4}
 \qquad(k\ge1).
\]
Reducing to a small multiplicative cycle exposes more structure than checking isolated numerical values; it also shows why the precise modulus is central to a divisibility question.

\section{Primitive roots give discrete-logarithm coordinates}

A primitive root modulo \(n\) is a generator of the unit group. If
\((\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z)^\times=\langle g\rangle\), then every unit has the form \(g^j\); multiplication becomes addition of exponents modulo \(\varphi(n)\). For odd primes \(p\), the unit group modulo \(p^r\) is cyclic, and a generator exists. For \(n=8\), however,
\[
 (\mathbb Z/8\mathbb Z)^\times=\{1,3,5,7\}
\]
and all nonidentity elements have square \(1\), so the group is not cyclic.

Apart from the trivial modulus \(1\), the classical classification is that primitive roots exist precisely for
\[
 n=2,\ 4,\ p^r,\ 2p^r
\]
with \(p\) an odd prime. A convenient primitive root is thus not available for every modulus. In other cases the Chinese remainder theorem and the structure theorem for finite abelian groups are the better language.

\section{Fourier Theory of the Unit Group}

A Dirichlet character modulo \(n\) is a homomorphism
\[
 \chi:(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z)^\times\longrightarrow\mathbb C^\times,
\]
extended to all integers by \(0\) on nonunits. Since the unit group is finite, the nonzero values of \(\chi\) are roots of unity. If \(G\) is a finite abelian group, its characters satisfy
\[
 \sum_{\chi\in\widehat G}\chi(g)
 =\begin{cases}|G|,&g=e,\\0,&g\ne e.\end{cases}
\]
To see the cancellation in the second line, choose a character \(\psi\) with \(\psi(g)\ne1\). Multiplying each summand by \(\psi(g)\) merely permutes the characters, so the sum equals \(\psi(g)\) times itself and must vanish.

For \(G=(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z)^\times\), this yields an indicator for a specified invertible congruence class:
\[
 \mathbf 1_{a\equiv b\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n)}
 =\frac1{\varphi(n)}
   \sum_{\chi\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n)}\chi(a)\overline{\chi(b)}
\]
when both \(a,b\) are units modulo \(n\). This is the exact connection between the original residue-class exercises and Fourier analysis on a finite group. It is also why characters enter the study of primes in arithmetic progressions.
