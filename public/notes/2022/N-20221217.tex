\notechapter{N-20221217}{Function Spaces: Domains, Orbit Symmetry, and Changing Numbers of Solutions}

The same function may be studied as a formula, as a map between topological spaces, or as an object carrying symmetry. A denominator determines where its formula makes sense; the image records attainable values; a functional equation can transport information across infinitely many inputs; a parameter can change the topology of a solution set. The following examples make these perspectives meet in actual calculations.

\section{Domains from Constraints}

For the real-valued expression
\[
 F(x)=\frac{\sqrt{x(3-x)}}{\log_{10}((x-3)^2)},
\]
the square root requires \(0\le x\le3\), the logarithm requires \(x\ne3\), and its value cannot be zero, excluding \((x-3)^2=1\). Hence
\[
 \operatorname{dom}F=[0,2)\cup(2,3).
\]
The open and closed endpoints encode different facts: \(x=0\) is allowed, while \(x=2\) gives a zero denominator and \(x=3\) gives an undefined logarithm.

A floor expression changes its form at integer thresholds:
\[
 \lfloor x\rfloor=k\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad k\le x<k+1.
\]
It is therefore often best handled on intervals with fixed \(k\). The discontinuities of the floor function are a geometric record of that case split.

\section{Ranges and Compactness}

Consider
\[
 G(x)=\sqrt{x-4}+\sqrt{15-3x},\qquad 4\le x\le5.
\]
With \(u=x-4\), this becomes
\[
 G=\sqrt u+\sqrt{3(1-u)},\qquad 0\le u\le1.
\]
Cauchy--Schwarz gives \(G^2\le(1+3)(u+1-u)=4\), and equality holds at \(u=1/4\), where \(G=2\). The only interior critical point is this maximum; the endpoint values are \(G(0)=\sqrt3\), \(G(1)=1\). Thus
\[
 \boxed{G([4,5])=[1,2].}
\]

There is a topological step hidden in the final interval. A continuous image of a compact interval is compact and connected, so once the extremal values are known the entire range between them is attained. On a noncompact or punctured domain, endpoint values might be approached without being attained.

\section{Absolute values are shadows of switching branches}

Knowing a range requires knowing where a function changes its formula. An especially useful example is the upper envelope
\[
 M(x)=\max\{(x-1)^2,(x+1)^2\}.
\]
Subtract the candidates: $(x+1)^2-(x-1)^2=4x$. Thus the second branch is active for $x\ge0$, the first for $x\le0$, and
\[
 \boxed{M(x)=x^2+1+2|x|.}
\]
The identity $\max\{u,v\}=(u+v+|u-v|)/2$ is a reliable way to derive such a formula, with no guesswork about the active branch.

For $x<0$ the derivative is $2x-2$, and for $x>0$ it is $2x+2$. At the minimum $x=0$, the one-sided derivatives are $-2$ and $2$: the ordinary derivative does not exist. Yet convexity provides a better replacement. A number $s$ is a \emph{subgradient} of $M$ at zero if
\[
 M(y)\ge M(0)+sy\quad\text{for every }y.
\]
Since $M(y)-M(0)=y^2+2|y|$, every $s\in[-2,2]$ satisfies the condition. If $|s|>2$, a sufficiently small $y$ with the same sign as $s$ violates it. Hence
\[
 \boxed{\partial M(0)=[-2,2].}
\]
The familiar smooth necessary condition $M'(x_*)=0$ becomes $0\in\partial M(x_*)$ for convex nonsmooth functions. Here the subgradient interval describes \emph{all} supporting slopes at the corner.\footnote{For maxima of differentiable convex functions and subgradients of active branches, see Boyd and Vandenberghe, \href{https://see.stanford.edu/materials/lsocoee364b/01-subgradients_notes.pdf}{\emph{Subgradients}, EE364b notes}.}

Switching can also change the number of solutions at one parameter value. The simpler function
\[
 H(x)=|x-1|+|x+1|=
 \begin{cases}
 -2x,&x<-1,\\
 2,&-1\le x\le1,\\
 2x,&x>1
 \end{cases}
\]
has no solutions to $H(x)=c$ for $c<2$, a whole \emph{interval} of solutions for $c=2$, and precisely two solutions $x=\pm c/2$ for $c>2$. A critical value can create an entire plateau, in contrast to the pair of roots created at the smooth tangency in the logarithmic example below.

\section{Dense Sets and Integrability}

Let \(D(x)=\mathbf1_{\mathbb Q}(x)\). Rationals and irrationals are dense in every interval, so \(D\) is discontinuous at every point. On any nontrivial closed interval, each subinterval has supremum \(1\) and infimum \(0\); its upper and lower Riemann sums differ by the whole interval length. Hence \(D\) is nowhere Riemann integrable on a nontrivial interval.

Yet \(\mathbb Q\) has Lebesgue measure zero, so its Lebesgue integral is zero:
\[
 \int_a^bD(x)\,dx=0
 \quad\text{in the Lebesgue sense}.
\]
This is a structural distinction between two integrals. Riemann sums examine the oscillation on every subinterval, while Lebesgue integration can disregard a measure-zero set.

Thomae's function makes the boundary sharper:
\[
 T(x)=
 \begin{cases}
 1/q,&x=p/q\text{ in lowest terms},\ q>0,\\
 0,&x\notin\mathbb Q.
 \end{cases}
\]
It is discontinuous exactly at the rational points and continuous at every irrational point. To see continuity at an irrational \(x_0\), fix \(\varepsilon>0\) and choose \(N\) with \(1/N<\varepsilon\). A bounded neighborhood contains only finitely many rationals with denominator \(q\le N\). Shrink the neighborhood to avoid them. All remaining values of \(T\) are less than \(\varepsilon\). Since the discontinuity set is countable, the Lebesgue criterion for Riemann integrability shows \(T\) is Riemann integrable with integral \(0\).

\section{A functional equation is transport along an orbit}

Suppose \(f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R\) satisfies
\[
 f(x+1)=1-f(x).
\]
Applying the shift twice gives \(f(x+2)=f(x)\). Every \(x\) has a unique decomposition \(x=t+n\) with \(0\le t<1\) and \(n\in\mathbb Z\). If \(h=f|_{[0,1)}\), then
\[
 f(t+n)=
 \begin{cases}
 h(t),&n\text{ even},\\
 1-h(t),&n\text{ odd}.
 \end{cases}
\]
Thus all solutions are determined by arbitrary data on a fundamental interval, transported by the integer translation action.

Continuity is an additional constraint. If \(h\) is continuous on \([0,1)\) and has a limit as \(t\uparrow1\), the extension is continuous on \(\mathbb R\) precisely when
\[
 \lim_{t\uparrow1}h(t)=1-h(0).
\]
For \(h(t)=2t-t^2\), both sides are \(1\), so the resulting period-two function is continuous.

Write \(g=f-\tfrac12\). Then \(g(x+1)=-g(x)\), an \emph{antiperiodicity} relation. If \(g\) is square-integrable on one period and expanded in the \(2\)-periodic Fourier basis \(e^{\pi ikx}\), the shift multiplies the \(k\)-th mode by \((-1)^k\). Antiperiodicity therefore forces the even Fourier coefficients to vanish. The alternating-value functional equation has become a spectral selection rule.

\section{A quadratic recurrence hides a circle-doubling map}

An iteration problem looks quite different from an ordinary range problem, but it becomes manageable when the coordinates expose the repeated operation. Consider
\[
 x_{n+1}=x_n^2-2,\qquad x_0\in[-2,2].
\]
Expanding $x_2=(x_0^2-2)^2-2$ is easy, but expanding $x_{10}$ is a hopeless way to understand its behavior. Instead write $x_0=2\cos\theta$, which is possible for every $x_0\in[-2,2]$. The double-angle identity gives
\[
 (2\cos\theta)^2-2
 =4\cos^2\theta-2
 =2\cos(2\theta).
\]
Applying the same calculation repeatedly proves by induction that
\[
 \boxed{x_n=2\cos(2^n\theta).}
\]
The quadratic recurrence has become the doubling of an angle on the circle. This is a \emph{semiconjugacy}
\[
 h(2\theta)=F(h(\theta)),\qquad
 h(\theta)=2\cos\theta,\quad F(x)=x^2-2.
\]
It is not a conjugacy: $\theta$ and $-\theta$ generally represent distinct angles but have the same image under $h$.

The trigonometric coordinate gives exact periodic orbits. Take
\[
 x_0=2\cos\frac{2\pi}{5}
 =\frac{\sqrt5-1}{2}.
\]
Then
\[
 x_1=2\cos\frac{4\pi}{5}
 =-\frac{\sqrt5+1}{2},
 \qquad
 x_2=2\cos\frac{8\pi}{5}=x_0.
\]
This is an exact two-cycle. The angle is multiplied by two each time, but taking the cosine forgets reflection of the angle; that is precisely why $8\pi/5$ and $2\pi/5$ give the same $x$.

The interval $[-2,2]$ is invariant because the cosine formula always stays inside it. Outside it, the behavior is completely different. If $x_0>2$, then
\[
 x_{n+1}-x_n=(x_n-2)(x_n+1)>0;
\]
a bounded increasing orbit would converge to a fixed point of $F$, but its only fixed points are $2$ and $-1$. Since every iterate is greater than two, the orbit must diverge to $+\infty$. If $x_0<-2$, the first iterate exceeds $2$, and the same conclusion follows.

This is a good example of a natural substitution doing more work than brute force. The algebraic recurrence, the geometry of the circle, and the distinction between bounded and escaping orbits all become explicit from a single identity. The relation with the doubling map is a standard example in one-dimensional dynamics; compare the discussion of the Chebyshev quadratic in \href{https://marksmath.org/classes/Fall2017ChaosAndFractals/chaos_and_fractals/section-chaos_and_doubling.html}{\emph{The Doubling Map and Chaos}}.

\section{Solving a reciprocal functional equation completely}

Suppose on \(\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}\) a function satisfies
\[
 f(x)=f(1)x+\frac{f(2)}x-1.
\]
Set \(A=f(1)\), \(B=f(2)\). Substituting \(x=1\) gives \(B=1\); substituting \(x=2\) then gives
\[
 1=2A+\frac12-1,\qquad A=\frac34.
\]
Consequently the function is uniquely determined:
\[
 \boxed{f(x)=\frac{3x}{4}+\frac1x-1.}
\]
On \(x>0\), AM--GM gives
\[
 f(x)\ge2\sqrt{\frac34}-1=\sqrt3-1,
\]
with equality when \(3x/4=1/x\), at \(x=2/\sqrt3\). A relation that initially seems to involve two unknown function values has reduced to two algebraic consistency equations.

\section{Tangency Changes Solution Counts}

For \(a>0\), consider
\[
 \log(ax)=2\log(x+1).
\]
The real domain is \(x>0\). Exponentiating yields
\[
 ax=(x+1)^2
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 a=x+2+\frac1x.
\]
The last expression is at least \(4\), with equality only at \(x=1\). Therefore there are no solutions when \(0<a<4\), exactly one when \(a=4\), and two when \(a>4\).

The same conclusion arises from the discriminant of \(x^2+(2-a)x+1\):
\[
 \Delta=a(a-4).
\]
But the function \(a(x)=x+2+1/x\) adds geometric information: the two branches coalesce at the nondegenerate minimum \(x=1\). Near the critical point,
\[
 a(x)=4+(x-1)^2+O((x-1)^3),
\]
so if \(a=4+\varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon>0\) small, the two solutions satisfy
\(x=1\pm\sqrt\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon)\). A discriminant-zero event is a simple fold bifurcation in the geometry of the zero set.

Domains and ranges determine where a function exists and which values it attains; the density of exceptional sets determines regularity and integrability; group actions transport functional equations; and critical values explain how solutions appear and disappear. Each viewpoint becomes useful exactly when the corresponding calculation demands it.
