\section{Finding a fixed point is only the beginning}

An iteration takes the form
\[
 a_{n+1}=f(a_n).
\]
If the sequence converges to \(L\) and \(f\) is continuous at \(L\), then
\[
 L=\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n+1}
  =\lim_{n\to\infty}f(a_n)=f(L).
\]
This necessary condition does not guarantee convergence. The map \(f(x)=-x\) fixes \(0\), yet almost every orbit alternates between \(a_1\) and \(-a_1\). The map \(f(x)=2x\) also fixes \(0\), but nonzero orbits move away from it.

For a differentiable map near a fixed point \(L\), the derivative is the first local test. By continuity of \(f'\), if \(|f'(L)|<1\) then one can find an interval about \(L\) in which \(f\) is a contraction, so iterations sufficiently close to \(L\) converge geometrically. If \(|f'(L)|>1\), the fixed point is locally repelling. The boundary case \(|f'(L)|=1\) requires more analysis and is particularly relevant to the Möbius example below. This local stability viewpoint is standard in one-dimensional discrete dynamics; the linear-recursion method later in the note follows MIT's \emph{Mathematics for Computer Science}, Chapter 10.\footnote{For the recurrence calculation see \href{https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-042j-mathematics-for-computer-science-fall-2010/resources/mit6_042jf10_chap10/}{MIT 6.042J, \emph{Mathematics for Computer Science}}, Chapter 10.}

An independent elementary proof method is monotone convergence. If one proves by induction that the iterates stay in a closed interval and that \(a_{n+1}\ge a_n\), boundedness gives a limit by completeness of the real numbers. Continuity then identifies the limit with a fixed point. Finding the fixed point without first proving these hypotheses would reverse the logical argument.

\section{Linearizing Möbius Recurrences}

A fractional-linear map is
\[
 F(x)=\frac{px+q}{rx+t},
 \qquad pt-qr\ne0.
\]
It is associated with the invertible matrix
\[
 M=\begin{pmatrix}p&q\\r&t\end{pmatrix}
\]
acting on the projective line. Iteration corresponds to the matrix powers \(M^n\) up to a common nonzero scalar. This gives an exact algebraic representation of a nonlinear-looking recurrence.

Fixed points satisfy
\[
 r x^2+(t-p)x-q=0.
\]
When this equation has two distinct finite roots \(\alpha,\beta\), the coordinate
\[
 h(x)=\frac{x-\alpha}{x-\beta}
\]
maps the fixed points to \(0\) and \(\infty\). The conjugate map \(h\circ F\circ h^{-1}\) fixes both \(0\) and \(\infty\), so it has the form \(u\mapsto\lambda u\). In particular,
\[
 \frac{a_{n+1}-\alpha}{a_{n+1}-\beta}
 =\lambda\,\frac{a_n-\alpha}{a_n-\beta}
\]
where the expressions are defined. The new sequence is geometric and its behavior can be read from \(|\lambda|\).

\begin{example}[A repeated fixed point reveals a finite-domain obstruction]
The original recurrence is
\[
 a_1=\frac12,\qquad a_{n+1}=\frac{5a_n-1}{a_n+3}.
\]
Its fixed-point equation reduces to
\[
 x^2-2x+1=(x-1)^2=0.
\]
The repeated fixed point is \(1\). The usual quotient
\((x-\alpha)/(x-\beta)\) degenerates, but the reciprocal coordinate
\[
 b_n=\frac1{a_n-1}
\]
gives a translation:
\[
 a_{n+1}-1=\frac{4(a_n-1)}{a_n+3},
 \qquad
 b_{n+1}=\frac{a_n+3}{4(a_n-1)}=b_n+\frac14.
\]
Since \(b_1=-2\), we obtain
\[
 b_n=-2+\frac{n-1}{4}=\frac{n-9}{4},
 \qquad
 \boxed{a_n=1+\frac4{n-9}.}
\]
There is an important restriction: the displayed real recurrence is defined only while the denominators are nonzero. In fact,
\[
 a_8=-3,
\]
so the rule for \(a_9\) requires division by \(0\). The original real-valued sequence stops at \(n=8\), and it would be incorrect to claim that the closed formula describes an infinite real sequence. On the projective line one can instead interpret \(a_9=\infty\) and continue with \(a_{10}=5\).

The derivative
\[
 F'(x)=\frac{16}{(x+3)^2}
\]
satisfies \(F'(1)=1\). The fixed point is therefore neutral at first order, which is precisely why the reciprocal coordinate and its additive drift are more informative than the elementary contraction test.
\end{example}

The single calculation brings together the two cases: distinct fixed points produce a multiplicative coordinate, while a repeated fixed point produces a translation in a suitable reciprocal coordinate. It also shows why the domain of iteration must be specified before asserting a limit.

\section{Repeated Roots of Linear Recurrences}

A linear recurrence
\[
 a_{n+2}=pa_{n+1}+qa_n
\]
has characteristic polynomial \(P(\lambda)=\lambda^2-p\lambda-q\). Substituting \(a_n=\lambda^n\) verifies why each nonzero characteristic root yields a geometric solution. If the two roots \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2\) are distinct, every solution has the form
\[
 a_n=A\lambda_1^n+B\lambda_2^n,
\]
with constants determined by two initial conditions.

For a repeated root \(\lambda\ne0\), a second independent solution is \(n\lambda^n\), yielding
\[
 a_n=(A+Bn)\lambda^n.
\]
The factor \(n\) is the discrete counterpart of a generalized eigenvector: the companion matrix
\[
 \begin{pmatrix}p&q\\1&0\end{pmatrix}
\]
has a Jordan block when the characteristic root repeats. Thus repeated roots are not merely an exception in a formula; they are a test of whether diagonalization succeeds.

The matrix picture also explains the Möbius case. Fractional-linear iteration uses a projective \(2\times2\) matrix, while a second-order linear recurrence uses a companion \(2\times2\) matrix. Both problems are governed by eigenvalues and invariant directions, but the transformation from a vector to its ratio changes the representation of the dynamics.

\section{Two Constructions of Spaces}

The original material also includes problems about Dedekind cuts and metric subspaces. These concern \emph{different} questions from recurrence linearization and deserve their own mathematical explanation rather than being forced into the recurrence story.

For a Dedekind cut \(X\subset\mathbb Q\), use the usual convention that \(X\) is a nonempty proper downward-closed set without a greatest element. For two cuts \(X,Y\), define
\[
 X+Y=\{x+y:x\in X,\ y\in Y\}.
\]
Why is this again a cut? It is nonempty by choosing \(x\in X,y\in Y\). It is downward closed: if \(q<x+y\), then
\(q=(q-y)+y\) with \(q-y<x\), hence \(q-y\in X\). It has no greatest element because \(X\) has none: for \(x+y\), choose \(x'>x\) still in \(X\). Finally, choose rational upper bounds \(B_X,B_Y\) of \(X,Y\); then every element of \(X+Y\) is less than \(B_X+B_Y\), proving it is proper. This checks the four cut axioms explicitly.

For a metric space \((X,d)\) and a subset \(Y\subseteq X\), the restriction
\[
 d_Y(y_1,y_2)=d(y_1,y_2)
\]
is again a metric, since each metric axiom holds for all points in \(X\), hence in \(Y\). In Euclidean space,
\[
 d(x,y)=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-y_i)^2},
\]
and the triangle inequality follows from the inner-product Cauchy--Schwarz inequality:
\[
 \|u+v\|_2^2
 =\|u\|_2^2+2\langle u,v\rangle+\|v\|_2^2
 \le(\|u\|_2+\|v\|_2)^2.
\]
These exercises establish how an existing order or distance structure can be extended or restricted, without pretending that the resulting objects are instances of the same iteration theorem.
