\notechapter{N-20221228}{Quotient and Product Topologies: Universal Properties and Gluing}

\section{Gluing Endpoints by Quotients}

Start with \(I=[0,1]\) and identify \(0\sim1\). The map
\[
 q:I\longrightarrow S^1,\qquad q(t)=e^{2\pi it}
\]
is continuous, surjective, and identifies exactly the two endpoints. It therefore induces a continuous bijection \(I/(0\sim1)\to S^1\). The quotient is compact and the circle is Hausdorff, so the induced bijection is a homeomorphism. This last compact-to-Hausdorff argument is the rigorous step behind the familiar picture of bending an interval into a circle.

The general definition for a surjection \(q:X\to Y\) is
\[
 U\subseteq Y\text{ is open}
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 q^{-1}(U)\subseteq X\text{ is open}.
\]
This is the finest topology on \(Y\) for which \(q\) is continuous. A map \(f:Y\to Z\) is continuous precisely when \(f\circ q:X\to Z\) is continuous. Both parts follow immediately by testing preimages of open sets in \(Z\).

A subset \(A\subseteq X\) is called \emph{saturated} when
\[
 A=q^{-1}(q(A)).
\]
Thus the open subsets of the quotient correspond to the saturated open subsets upstairs. Near the point obtained from identifying \(0\) and \(1\), a neighborhood pulls back to the union of small half-intervals near \emph{both} ends. Keeping only one end is not saturated and therefore does not describe a quotient neighborhood.

The presentation and notation follow the quotient-space and product-space chapters in Munkres' \emph{Topology}, with the universal-property formulation also given in Hatcher's \emph{Algebraic Topology}, Section 0.\footnote{See A. Hatcher, \emph{Algebraic Topology}, Section 0, freely available at \url{https://pi.math.cornell.edu/~hatcher/AT/AT.pdf}.}

\section{Collapsing a boundary and reading a glued surface}

The quotient \(D^n/S^{n-1}\) collapses the boundary of the closed \(n\)-disk to a single point. The resulting space is homeomorphic to \(S^n\): the disk interior maps onto a sphere minus one point, while its boundary maps to the missing point. This can be described by stereographic projection, or by regarding the disk as the one-point compactification of its interior \(\mathbb R^n\). The map is a quotient map because the compact disk maps onto the Hausdorff sphere.

A square provides another family of examples. Identifying one pair of opposite edges with matching directions gives a cylinder; identifying the other pair in the same direction then gives a torus. Reversing one gluing leads to a Klein bottle, whereas a single twisted identification on a strip gives a Möbius band. It is essential to specify \emph{which} edges and the direction of their identifications; a sketch with unlabeled arrows is not enough.

The same gluing can be encoded by an attaching word. For a torus it is
\[
 aba^{-1}b^{-1},
\]
while a Klein bottle can be presented by
\[
 aba^{-1}b.
\]
For the torus, a square is attached as a two-cell to a wedge of two circles. Its boundary loop is a commutator, yielding
\[
 \pi_1(T^2)\cong
 \langle a,b\mid aba^{-1}b^{-1}=1\rangle
 \cong\mathbb Z^2.
\]
Thus the arrow labels in the square are the start of a genuine algebraic calculation of a fundamental group.

\section{Products use the opposite continuity test}

For topological spaces \(X,Y\), the product topology on \(X\times Y\) is generated by the rectangles
\[
 U\times V,\qquad U\subseteq X,\;V\subseteq Y\text{ open}.
\]
Indeed,
\[
 U\times V=\pi_X^{-1}(U)\cap\pi_Y^{-1}(V),
\]
so it is the coarsest topology making both projections \(\pi_X,\pi_Y\) continuous. Its mapping property reads
\[
 h:Z\to X\times Y\text{ continuous}
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \pi_X\circ h,\ \pi_Y\circ h\text{ continuous}.
\]
Compare this with the quotient criterion, where the universal test concerns \emph{maps out of} the quotient. The two constructions are both specified by functions, but by different directions of functions:
\[
 \begin{array}{c|c|c}
 \text{construction}&\text{canonical maps}&\text{continuity checked by}\\ \hline
 \text{product}&X\times Y\to X,Y&\text{component maps into the product}\\
 \text{quotient}&X\to X/{\sim}&\text{maps defined on the quotient}
 \end{array}
\]
A continuous map into a product requires coordinatewise continuity; a continuous map out of a quotient can be checked before identifying the points.

\section{When quotients behave badly}

Let \(q:\mathbb R\to Y\) identify all rational numbers as one point and all irrational numbers as another. Neither \(\mathbb Q\) nor its complement is open in \(\mathbb R\), and neither has open nonempty saturated proper subsets. Therefore \(Y\) carries the \emph{indiscrete} two-point topology. It cannot be Hausdorff. This is a concrete warning that quotient spaces of Hausdorff spaces need not remain Hausdorff.

There is a second pitfall in mixing the two constructions. Given quotient maps
\[
 q:X\to X/{\sim},\qquad r:Y\to Y/{\approx},
\]
the product \(q\times r:X\times Y\to(X/{\sim})\times(Y/{\approx})\) is a continuous surjection, but it need not be a quotient map for arbitrary spaces. The induced set-theoretic bijection
\[
 (X\times Y)/({\sim}\times{\approx})
 \longrightarrow (X/{\sim})\times(Y/{\approx})
\]
may therefore fail to be a homeomorphism. If both \(q\) and \(r\) are \emph{open quotient maps}, however, their product is open and surjective, hence a quotient map; the displayed bijection is then a homeomorphism. The endpoint-identification map above is closed rather than necessarily open, but compactness/Hausdorffness supplies an alternative direct proof for the torus.

The point of the universal-property approach is that it forces these hypotheses into view. A drawing can correctly predict a circle or torus while hiding the conditions under which its proposed quotient carries the intended topology.
