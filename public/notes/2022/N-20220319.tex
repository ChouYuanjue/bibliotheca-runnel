\notechapter{N-20220319}{Sequences, Limits, Derivatives, and Riemann Integration}

A limit identifies which changes eventually become negligible; a derivative selects the best linear approximation; an integral accumulates such changes. This development follows Rudin's \emph{Principles of Mathematical Analysis} and MIT 18.100B real-analysis notes, with each conclusion tied to its hypotheses.\footnote{\url{https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-100b-real-analysis-spring-2025/pages/lecture-notes/}}

\section{A limit is an eventually quantified statement}

For a sequence \(a:\mathbb N\to\mathbb R\), write \(a_n\to\ell\) when
\[
 \forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists N\in\mathbb N\
 \forall n\ge N:\quad |a_n-\ell|<\varepsilon.
\]
The cutoff \(N\) depends on the requested error but must work for every later index. For \(a_n=n/(n+1)\), we have \(|a_n-1|=1/(n+1)\); choosing \(N+1>1/\varepsilon\) establishes convergence.

By contrast, \((-1)^n\) cannot converge. Both its even subsequence, constantly \(1\), and its odd subsequence, constantly \(-1\), would have to approach the same limit. Uniqueness of limits contradicts this.

\section{Limits ignore the value at the point}

Let \(a\) be an accumulation point of the domain \(A\). The statement \(\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L\) means
\[
 \forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists\delta>0:
 \quad x\in A,\quad 0<|x-a|<\delta
 \Longrightarrow |f(x)-L|<\varepsilon.
\]
The puncture is essential. If \(f(x)=x^2\) for \(x\ne2\), but \(f(2)=17\), then \(\lim_{x\to2}f(x)=4\), even though \(f(2)=17\).

\begin{theorem}[Sequential criterion]
The limit \(f(x)\to L\) as \(x\to a\) exists if and only if every sequence \(x_n\in A\setminus\{a\}\) with \(x_n\to a\) satisfies \(f(x_n)\to L\).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The forward direction follows by applying the \(\delta\) condition once \(x_n\) is close to \(a\). Conversely, if no suitable \(\delta\) exists for some \(\varepsilon_0>0\), choose for each \(n\) a point \(x_n\in A\) such that
\[
 0<|x_n-a|<1/n,\qquad |f(x_n)-L|\ge\varepsilon_0.
\]
Then \(x_n\to a\), but its image does not converge to \(L\), a contradiction.
\end{proof}

This proves discontinuity of the rational-indicator function at every real point: rational and irrational sequences converge to any chosen point but have constantly different function values.

\section{The derivative is a linear approximation}

A function \(f\) is differentiable at \(a\) if
\[
 f'(a)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}h
\]
exists. Equivalently,
\[
 f(a+h)=f(a)+f'(a)h+o(|h|),
\]
where \(r(h)=o(|h|)\) means \(r(h)/|h|\to0\). The linear part \(f'(a)h\) is unique, because two proposed coefficients would differ by \(o(|h|)/h\to0\).

For \(f(x)=x^2\),
\[
 f(a+h)=a^2+2ah+h^2=a^2+2ah+o(|h|).
\]
The little-o term measures the actual approximation error. By contrast \(r(h)=O(h^2)\) asserts only that \(|r(h)|\le Ch^2\) near zero, a stronger scale-specific bound in this example.

The product rule follows from the exact identity
\[
 u(a+h)v(a+h)-u(a)v(a)
 =u(a+h)(v(a+h)-v(a))+v(a)(u(a+h)-u(a)).
\]
Divide by \(h\) and use differentiability of both factors to obtain
\[
 (uv)'=u'v+uv'.
\]
Applying this to \(v(1/v)=1\) gives the quotient rule when \(v\ne0\). Composing two first-order expansions gives the chain rule \((F\circ g)'=F'(g)g'\). Repeated product differentiation leads by induction to
\[
 (uv)^{(n)}=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk u^{(k)}v^{(n-k)}.
\]

\section{An integral is a partition-independent sum}

For \(P:a=x_0<\cdots<x_m=b\), select \(\xi_i\in[x_{i-1},x_i]\) and define
\[
 S(f,P,\xi)=\sum_{i=1}^m f(\xi_i)(x_i-x_{i-1}).
\]
Riemann integrability requires these sums to approach one limit as the maximum interval length tends to zero, independently of the sample points. Every continuous function on a compact interval is integrable.

For \(f(x)=x\) on \([0,1]\), equal partitions and right-endpoint samples give
\[
 S_n=\frac1{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^ni
 =\frac{n(n+1)}{2n^2}\to\frac12.
\]
This computes the value once integrability has been established by continuity.

\section{The fundamental theorem connects both operations}

\begin{theorem}[Fundamental theorem of calculus]
For continuous \(f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R\), the function
\[
 F(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt
\]
has derivative \(F'(x)=f(x)\) at interior points. If \(G'=f\), then \(\int_a^b f(t)\,dt=G(b)-G(a)\).
\end{theorem}

For \(h>0\),
\[
 \frac{F(x+h)-F(x)}h=\frac1h\int_x^{x+h}f(t)\,dt.
\]
The difference from \(f(x)\) has absolute value at most \(\sup_{|t-x|\le h}|f(t)-f(x)|\), which tends to zero by continuity. The case \(h<0\) is analogous. The second identity follows from the first because \(F-G\) has derivative zero on the connected interval, and the mean value theorem makes it constant.

\section{What changes when one changes the norm}

Metric convergence replaces \(|a_n-a|\) by \(d(a_n,a)\). A useful example is \(f_n(x)=x^n\) on \([0,1]\). Although
\[
 \|f_n\|_{L^1}=\int_0^1x^n\,dx=\frac1{n+1}\to0,
\]
its supremum norm is \(\|f_n\|_\infty=1\) for every \(n\). Thus it converges to zero in \(L^1\) but not uniformly. Different notions of distance ask for different notions of small error, even though every convergence proof follows the same epsilon-quantifier pattern.
