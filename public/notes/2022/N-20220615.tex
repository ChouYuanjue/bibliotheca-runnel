\notechapter{N-20220615}{Geometry Under Constraints: Projections, Packing, and Symmetric Coordinates}

A geometric extremum depends as much on the feasible set as on the formula being optimized. Squared distances invite orthogonal projection; circle packing requires distance constraints between centers; symmetric rational equations become simpler after removing an irrelevant common scale. Each situation suggests a different set of coordinates.

\section{A distance problem is a projection problem}

Let \(A=(0,-2)\), \(B=(2,-2)\), and let \(M=(x,y)\) lie on \(x+y=1\). We want to minimize
\[
 |M-A|^2+|M-B|^2.
\]
Put \(C=(A+B)/2=(1,-2)\). Expanding inner products gives the exact identity
\[
 \boxed{|M-A|^2+|M-B|^2
       =2|M-C|^2+\frac12|A-B|^2.}
\]
It holds in any inner-product space, not just in the plane. The distance \(|A-B|=2\) is fixed, so minimizing the sum is equivalent to projecting \(C\) orthogonally onto the constraint line.

The line has normal vector \(n=(1,1)\). Its signed equation is \(n\cdot M=1\), while \(n\cdot C=-1\). Therefore the projection is
\[
 M_*=C+\frac{1-n\cdot C}{\|n\|^2}n
 =(1,-2)+(1,1)=(2,-1).
\]
The squared distance from \(C\) to the line equals \(2\). Hence
\[
 \min_{x+y=1}(|M-A|^2+|M-B|^2)=2\cdot2+2=6.
\]
There is no maximum because the constraint line is unbounded and the squared distance grows quadratically along it.

This same identity underlies least squares: minimizing sums of squared distances amounts to projecting onto an affine subspace, with the residual orthogonal to all allowed directions.

\section{The Parabola's Farthest Point}

Take a downward-opening parabola
\[
 y=ax^2+bx,\qquad a<0,\ b>0,
\]
restricted to the interval between its two \(x\)-intercepts, \(0\le x\le-b/a\). The squared distance to the origin is
\[
 F(x)=x^2+(ax^2+bx)^2,
 \qquad F'(x)=2x+2(ax^2+bx)(2ax+b).
\]
Although the height of the parabola is maximal at \(x=-b/(2a)\), the distance to the origin also depends on \(x^2\). The vertex therefore need not maximize \(F\).

For the concrete parabola \(y=2x-x^2\) on \([0,2]\),
\[
 F(x)=x^2+x^2(2-x)^2,\qquad
 F'(x)=2x(2x^2-6x+5).
\]
The quadratic factor has discriminant \(36-40=-4\) and positive leading coefficient, so \(F'(x)>0\) for \(0<x\le2\). Thus the farthest point is \((2,0)\), at squared distance \(4\), while the vertex \((1,1)\) has squared distance only \(2\).

An extremum of one coordinate is not generally an extremum of a nonlinear function of all coordinates. The correct objective must be differentiated on the actual feasible domain.

\section{Packing circles becomes a configuration-space problem}

Suppose \(n\) closed disks of radius \(1/2\) must fit inside a container \(C\subseteq\mathbb R^2\). Instead of manipulating the disks directly, represent each by its center \(c_i\). The conditions are
\[
 \|c_i-c_j\|\ge1\quad(i\ne j),\qquad
 c_i\in C\ominus B_{1/2},
\]
where \(C\ominus B_{1/2}\) is the set of points whose radius-\(1/2\) disk is wholly contained in \(C\). This operation is called morphological erosion or Minkowski subtraction.

For two unit-diameter disks inside a round container of radius \(R\), both centers must lie within radius \(R-\tfrac12\) of its center. Their greatest possible separation is \(2R-1\), which must be at least \(1\). Thus \(R\ge1\), and placing the disk centers at opposite ends of a diameter attains equality. The minimal container diameter is exactly \(2\).

For two disks inside a square of side \(s\), each center must lie in the smaller square of side \(s-1\). The largest possible distance between two such centers is \(\sqrt2(s-1)\). Hence
\[
 \sqrt2(s-1)\ge1
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \boxed{s\ge1+\frac1{\sqrt2}.}
\]
Opposite corners of the allowed center square achieve the bound.

Already for \(n=2\), the exact results are much stronger than the area comparison \(n\pi/4\le\operatorname{area}(C)\). For larger \(n\), the feasible configurations form a constrained subset of \(C^n\) from which all pairwise collision regions have been removed. This configuration-space geometry, rather than area alone, explains the difficulty of optimal packings.

\section{Three equal disks force an equilateral configuration}

The two-disk examples reveal the constraints, but a third disk makes the geometry more interesting. What is the smallest circular container that can hold three disks of radius $1/2$ without overlap? A naive area estimate would only give
\[
  \pi R^2\ge3\pi(1/2)^2,
  \qquad R\ge\frac{\sqrt3}{2},
\]
which is far from sharp because it ignores how centers are separated.

Write $c_1,c_2,c_3$ for the disk centers, measured from the container center, and let
\[
 r=R-\frac12.
\]
Containment implies $\|c_i\|\le r$, while nonoverlap implies $\|c_i-c_j\|\ge1$ for each pair $i\ne j$. The key is a simple identity:
\[
 \boxed{\sum_{i<j}\|c_i-c_j\|^2
 =3\sum_{i=1}^3\|c_i\|^2-
    \left\|\sum_{i=1}^3c_i\right\|^2.}
\]
Expand the squares to verify it; each $\|c_i\|^2$ occurs twice on the left, and each pairwise dot product occurs with coefficient $-2$. The right-hand side rearranges exactly those terms.

The three separation constraints give a lower bound of $3$ for the left side. For the right side,
\[
 3\le\sum_{i<j}\|c_i-c_j\|^2
 \le3\sum_i\|c_i\|^2
 \le9r^2.
\]
Therefore $r\ge1/\sqrt3$, or
\[
 \boxed{R\ge\frac12+\frac1{\sqrt3}.}
\]
This lower bound is achieved. Place the three centers at the vertices of an equilateral triangle of side $1$, centered at the origin. Each center lies at distance $1/\sqrt3$ from the origin, so the outer container of radius $1/2+1/\sqrt3$ touches all three disks while the disks touch one another.

The inequality is stronger than an area bound because it keeps a \emph{pairwise distance invariant}. Equality in the algebra forces all centers onto the allowed circle, their vector sum to vanish, and all pairwise separations to equal one. Thus the symmetric arrangement is not merely a plausible picture; the calculation explains the rigidity of the optimizer.

\section{Symmetric rational equations live on projective ratios}

Consider positive \(a,b,c\) satisfying
\[
 \frac a{b+c}+\frac b{c+a}+\frac c{a+b}=4.
\]
If \((a,b,c)\) solves the equation, so does \((ta,tb,tc)\) for every \(t>0\). Hence the equation constrains only the ratios among \(a,b,c\). It is natural to introduce the elementary symmetric quantities
\[
 s=a+b+c,\qquad p=ab+bc+ca,\qquad q=abc.
\]
Because \(\tfrac a{b+c}=\tfrac{s}{s-a}-1\), the original equation becomes
\[
 s\left(\frac1{s-a}+\frac1{s-b}+\frac1{s-c}\right)=7.
\]
The pairwise products of the denominators sum to \(s^2+p\), while their product equals \(sp-q\). Clearing the strictly positive denominator yields the homogeneous cubic
\[
 \boxed{s^3-6sp+7q=0.}
\]

There is an actual family of positive solutions, not just a formal projective equation. Fix the overall scale by taking \(b=c=1\) and write \(a=t>0\). The original condition becomes
\[
 \frac t2+\frac2{t+1}=4.
\]
Clearing the positive denominator gives
\[
 t(t+1)+4=8(t+1),
 \qquad t^2-7t-4=0.
\]
The unique positive solution on this symmetric ray is
\[
 \boxed{t=\frac{7+\sqrt{65}}2.}
\]
The other quadratic root is negative. Thus all positive triples proportional to
\[
 \left(\frac{7+\sqrt{65}}2,\ 1,\ 1\right)
\]
satisfy the rational equation. The cubic derived in \(s,p,q\) gives the same result: when \(s=t+2,\ p=2t+1,\ q=t\), its left side factors as
\[
 s^3-6sp+7q=(t+1)(t^2-7t-4).
\]
The factor \(t+1\) is never zero in the positive region. This explicit section of the projective solution set illustrates why removing scale helps: one homogeneous equation in three variables becomes a scalar quadratic after intersecting with a geometrically meaningful symmetry ray.

This equation defines a projective cubic locus in the ratios \([a:b:c]\), restricted to the positive region. An elementary inequality still gives a useful geometric boundary: by Nesbitt's inequality,
\[
 \frac a{b+c}+\frac b{c+a}+\frac c{a+b}\ge\frac32,
\]
with equality at \(a=b=c\). The level \(4\) therefore lies well away from the balanced point.

The projection calculation, the disk-center constraints, and the homogeneous cubic all remove irrelevant coordinates. For squared distances we eliminate translation using a midpoint; for packing we replace congruent disks by their centers; for a symmetric rational equation we quotient out overall scale. In each case the improved coordinates reveal the geometry of the feasible set before optimization begins.
