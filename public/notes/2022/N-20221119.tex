\notechapter{N-20221119}{Prime Factorization, Divisor Sums, and Arithmetic Functions}

The elementary statement that every positive integer factors uniquely into primes has surprisingly different consequences: it gives algorithms, product formulas for divisor counts, inversion formulas, and eventually Euler products. A useful thread is that prime exponents turn multiplication into addition, divisor sums into algebraic convolutions, and finally arithmetic functions into generating series.\footnote{Compare \href{https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-781-theory-of-numbers-spring-2012/pages/lecture-notes/}{MIT 18.781, \emph{Theory of Numbers}, lecture notes}.}

\section{A divisor below a square root}

If an integer \(n>1\) is composite, write \(n=ab\) with \(1<a\le b\). Then \(a^2\le n\), hence \(a\le\sqrt n\). Factoring \(a\) supplies a prime divisor \(p\le\sqrt n\). Thus it suffices to test primes no larger than \(\sqrt n\) to decide whether \(n\) is prime.

For \(n=221\), it is enough to test the primes \(2,3,5,7,11,13\), because \(\sqrt{221}<15\). The factor \(13\) succeeds:
\[
 221=13\cdot17.
\]
Trial division and the sieve of Eratosthenes exploit the same bound in different ways: the sieve marks composites in a whole interval, whereas trial division tests one integer.

\section{Euclid's theorem and the prime-divisor lemma}

\begin{theorem}[Euclid]
There are infinitely many prime numbers.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If \(p_1,\ldots,p_k\) were all the primes, the integer \(N=p_1\cdots p_k+1>1\) would have a prime divisor \(q\). But \(N\equiv1\pmod{p_i}\) for every \(i\), so \(q\) is absent from the supposedly complete list.
\end{proof}

To obtain \emph{uniqueness} of prime factorization, we need another fact.

\begin{lemma}[Euclid's lemma]
If \(p\) is prime and \(p\mid ab\), then \(p\mid a\) or \(p\mid b\).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
If \(p\nmid a\), then \(\gcd(a,p)=1\). Bezout's identity supplies integers \(u,v\) with \(ua+vp=1\). Multiplying by \(b\) gives \(uab+vpb=b\), whose left side is divisible by \(p\) because \(p\mid ab\). Hence \(p\mid b\).
\end{proof}

Repeated division proves the existence of a prime factorization. To prove uniqueness, compare two factorizations of the same integer. Euclid's lemma forces the first prime in one factorization to divide a prime in the other and hence to equal it. Cancel it, and continue by induction.

\section{Prime exponents as arithmetic coordinates}

For \(n>0\), let \(v_p(n)\) denote the exponent of a prime \(p\) in \(n\). Unique factorization is the assertion that
\[
 n=\prod_p p^{v_p(n)},\qquad v_p(n)\ge0,
\]
with finitely many nonzero exponents. It turns multiplication and divisibility into coordinatewise statements:
\[
 v_p(mn)=v_p(m)+v_p(n),\qquad
 a\mid b\Longleftrightarrow v_p(a)\le v_p(b)\quad\forall p.
\]
Consequently the greatest common divisor and least common multiple satisfy
\[
 v_p(\gcd(a,b))=\min(v_p(a),v_p(b)),\quad
 v_p(\operatorname{lcm}(a,b))=\max(v_p(a),v_p(b)).
\]
For example, \(72=2^3 3^2\), \(90=2\cdot3^2\cdot5\), so
\[
 \gcd(72,90)=2\cdot 3^2=18,\qquad
 \operatorname{lcm}(72,90)=2^3 3^2 5=360.
\]

\section{Counting divisors and their sum}

If \(n=\prod_{i=1}^r p_i^{a_i}\), every divisor has the unique form \(d=\prod_i p_i^{b_i}\) with \(0\le b_i\le a_i\). Choosing each \(b_i\) independently gives
\[
 \tau(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}1=\prod_{i=1}^r(a_i+1).
\]
Summing the values of all choices instead gives
\[
 \sigma(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}d
 =\prod_{i=1}^r(1+p_i+\cdots+p_i^{a_i})
 =\prod_i\frac{p_i^{a_i+1}-1}{p_i-1}.
\]

For \(n=12=2^2\cdot3\), the divisors are \(1,2,3,4,6,12\), so \(\tau(12)=6\) and \(\sigma(12)=28\). The formulas agree:
\[
 (2+1)(1+1)=6,\qquad (1+2+4)(1+3)=28.
\]
A positive integer \(n\) is a perfect square exactly when all its \(v_p(n)\) are even; by the formula for \(\tau\), this is equivalent to \(\tau(n)\) being odd. Likewise, \(n\) is called perfect when \(\sigma(n)=2n\), as for \(n=6\).

\section{Legendre's formula is a double count}

The exponent of \(p\) in \(n!\) is
\[
 \boxed{v_p(n!)=\sum_{j\ge1}\left\lfloor\frac n{p^j}\right\rfloor.}
\]
Each multiple of \(p\) contributes at least one factor; each multiple of \(p^2\) contributes one additional factor, and so on. For example,
\[
 v_2(10!)=\lfloor10/2\rfloor+\lfloor10/4\rfloor+
 \lfloor10/8\rfloor=5+2+1=8.
\]
This also yields a practical test for binomial coefficients:
\[
 v_p\binom nk=v_p(n!)-v_p(k!)-v_p((n-k)!).
\]
The integrality of \(\binom nk\) is reflected in the nonnegativity of every one of these prime exponents.

\section{Dirichlet convolution encodes divisor sums}

An arithmetic function assigns a complex number to each positive integer. For such functions define
\[
 (f*g)(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}f(d)g(n/d).
\]
The function \(\varepsilon(1)=1\), \(\varepsilon(n)=0\) for \(n>1\) is the identity under convolution. If \(\mathbf1(n)=1\) and \(\mathrm{id}(n)=n\), then
\[
 \tau=\mathbf1*\mathbf1,\qquad
 \sigma=\mathbf1*\mathrm{id}.
\]
The first identity counts one ordered pair of divisors \(d,e\) with \(de=n\) for each divisor \(d\). The second weighs each pair by \(e\); it computes precisely the divisor sum.

Associativity is worth checking rather than assuming. Both \((f*g)*h\) and \(f*(g*h)\) sum \(f(a)g(b)h(c)\) over the same ordered triples \(abc=n\).

\section{Möbius inversion removes previous divisor sums}

Define the Möbius function by
\[
 \mu(n)=
 \begin{cases}
 1,&n=1,\\
 (-1)^r,&n\text{ is the product of }r\text{ distinct primes},\\
 0,&p^2\mid n\text{ for some prime }p.
 \end{cases}
\]
For \(n>1\) with \(r\) distinct prime factors,
\[
 \sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)=\sum_{j=0}^r\binom rj(-1)^j=(1-1)^r=0.
\]
For \(n=1\) the sum is \(1\). Thus
\[
 \mu*\mathbf1=\varepsilon.
\]
If \(F(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}f(d)=(\mathbf1*f)(n)\), convolution by \(\mu\) gives the \emph{inversion formula}
\[
 f(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)F(n/d).
\]

\begin{example}
The integers \(1\le k\le n\) with \(\gcd(k,n)=1\) are counted by Euler's totient \(\varphi(n)\). Partitioning \(1,\ldots,n\) by their gcd with \(n\) gives
\[
 \sum_{d\mid n}\varphi(d)=n.
\]
Möbius inversion yields
\[
 \varphi(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)\frac nd
 =n\prod_{p\mid n}\left(1-\frac1p\right).
\]
For \(n=12\), this predicts \(\varphi(12)=12(1-\tfrac12)(1-\tfrac13)=4\), matching the units \(1,5,7,11\) modulo \(12\).
\end{example}

\section{Euler Products and Factorization}

For \(\Re(s)>1\), the series
\[
 \zeta(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{-s}
\]
converges absolutely. Expanding geometric series for every prime \(p\) and using unique factorization gives
\[
 \boxed{\zeta(s)=\prod_p(1-p^{-s})^{-1}\qquad(\Re(s)>1).}
\]
The region of convergence matters: no unrestricted interchange of infinitely many sums and products has been justified outside it.

The simple congruence exercise \(2,4,8,6\pmod{10}\) belongs to another consequence of the same arithmetic structure: units modulo \(m\) form a finite multiplicative group. A periodicity claim about powers must therefore be checked against coprimality. For example, \(2\) is not a unit modulo \(10\), so its observed eventual cycle is not an instance of Euler's theorem on \((\mathbb Z/10\mathbb Z)^\times\).

The progression is from a local divisibility test to unique prime coordinates, then to product formulas and inversion. Each new operation is motivated by a concrete earlier calculation; analytic continuation or deeper zeta-function claims can be studied separately.
