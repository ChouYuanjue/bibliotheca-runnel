\notechapter{N-20221210}{Binomial Coefficients as a Calculus of Finite Choices}

The binomial coefficient \(\binom nk\) counts the \(k\)-element subsets of an \(n\)-element set. That familiar count becomes much more powerful when it is treated as a change of coordinates: a function of an integer can be expanded in the basis \(\binom xk\), just as a smooth function is expanded in powers of \(x\). The inverse operation is a finite difference, and the same subset counting leads to inclusion--exclusion, probability, and labelled combinatorial structures.\footnote{The binomial-basis and combinatorial generating-function perspective follows Stanley, \emph{Enumerative Combinatorics}, Vol. 1, Chapters 1 and 3, and Flajolet--Sedgewick, \emph{Analytic Combinatorics}, Chapter II: \url{https://algo.inria.fr/flajolet/Publications/book.pdf}.}

\section{The binomial expansion is a decomposition into choices}

Expanding \((a+b)^n\) means choosing either \(a\) or \(b\) from each of \(n\) factors. If exactly \(k\) factors contribute \(a\), the resulting monomial is \(a^kb^{n-k}\), and there are \(\binom nk\) choices. Therefore
\[
 \boxed{(a+b)^n=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk a^kb^{n-k}.}
\]
Putting \(a=b=1\) gives \(\sum_k\binom nk=2^n\): every subset is counted once. Putting \(a=1,b=-1\) gives, for \(n\ge1\),
\[
 \sum_{k=0}^n(-1)^k\binom nk=0.
\]
Thus the even-sized and odd-sized subsets are equally numerous. There is also a direct bijection: choose a fixed element and toggle whether it belongs to the subset, thereby reversing its cardinality's parity.

The triangle inequality yields the useful bound
\[
 |a+b|^n\le(|a|+|b|)^n
 =\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk|a|^k|b|^{n-k}.
\]
The validity of the inequality does not depend on formally moving absolute values through an equality; it follows from the ordinary triangle inequality applied to \(a+b\).

\section{Inclusion--exclusion is a binomial cancellation}

For finitely many sets \(A_1,\ldots,A_n\) inside a finite universe \(U\),
\[
 \left|\bigcup_{i=1}^nA_i\right|
 =\sum_{\varnothing\ne I\subseteq[n]}
     (-1)^{|I|+1}\left|\bigcap_{i\in I}A_i\right|.
\]
To prove it, fix \(u\in U\) belonging to exactly \(r\) of these sets. It is counted on the right with total weight
\[
 \sum_{k=1}^{r}(-1)^{k+1}\binom rk
 =1-(1-1)^r.
\]
The weight is \(1\) if \(r\ge1\) and \(0\) if \(r=0\), exactly the indicator of membership in the union.

This computation is the elementary core of Möbius inversion on the Boolean lattice of subsets: alternate signs cancel all the overcounted choices.

\section{Binomial inversion recovers hidden coefficients}

Suppose two sequences are related by
\[
 b_n=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk a_k.
\]
Then the inverse transform is
\[
 \boxed{a_n=\sum_{k=0}^n(-1)^{n-k}\binom nk b_k.}
\]
To verify it, substitute the first relation into the second and reverse the finite sums:
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \sum_{k=0}^n(-1)^{n-k}\binom nk b_k
 &=\sum_{j=0}^na_j
   \sum_{k=j}^n(-1)^{n-k}\binom nk\binom kj\\
 &=\sum_{j=0}^na_j\binom nj
   \sum_{r=0}^{n-j}(-1)^{n-j-r}\binom{n-j}r.
\end{aligned}
\]
The last inner sum is \((1-1)^{n-j}\). Only the term \(j=n\) survives, giving \(a_n\).

For instance, if \(b_n=2^n\), the inverse formula yields
\[
 a_n=\sum_{k=0}^n(-1)^{n-k}\binom nk2^k=(2-1)^n=1.
\]
The direct transform of the constant sequence \(a_k=1\) is indeed \(2^n\).

\section{Finite differences take the place of derivatives}

For a function \(f:\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\to\mathbb R\), define its forward difference
\[
 \Delta f(n)=f(n+1)-f(n).
\]
Iterating produces
\[
 \boxed{\Delta^kf(0)
 =\sum_{j=0}^k(-1)^{k-j}\binom kj f(j).}
\]
The coefficients are the same as in binomial inversion. The analogy with differentiation becomes exact on the binomial polynomials
\[
 \binom{x}{k}=\frac{x(x-1)\cdots(x-k+1)}{k!}.
\]
Pascal's identity says
\[
 \Delta\binom{x}{k}=\binom{x}{k-1},
\]
where \(\binom{x}{0}=1\). Thus the binomial basis is adapted to discrete differentiation, much as \(x^k/k!\) is adapted to ordinary differentiation.

If \(f(x)\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(d\), its Newton expansion is
\[
 \boxed{f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^d\Delta^kf(0)\binom{x}{k}.}
\]
Indeed, successive finite differences of this expression at \(0\) agree with those of \(f\); two polynomials of degree at most \(d\) with the same first \(d+1\) Newton coefficients are identical.

Take \(f(x)=x^3\). Directly,
\[
 \Delta f(0)=1,\quad
 \Delta^2f(0)=6,\quad
 \Delta^3f(0)=6.
\]
Hence
\[
 \boxed{x^3=\binom{x}{1}+6\binom{x}{2}+6\binom{x}{3}.}
\]
This identity is a discrete analogue of Taylor's theorem. It expresses the cubic in basis functions that respond to \(\Delta\) by lowering their index.

\section{Summation is the inverse of taking differences}

Pascal's identity also gives
\[
 \binom{k+1}{r+1}-\binom{k}{r+1}=\binom{k}{r}.
\]
Summing from \(k=0\) to \(n-1\) telescopes:
\[
 \boxed{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom{k}{r}=\binom{n}{r+1}.}
\]
This is the hockey-stick identity. In discrete calculus, the binomial polynomial \(\binom{x}{r+1}\) plays the role of an antiderivative of \(\binom{x}{r}\).

Combining this with the Newton expansion immediately evaluates sums of polynomial sequences. Using the cubic identity above,
\[
 \sum_{k=0}^{n-1}k^3
 =\binom n2+6\binom n3+6\binom n4
 =\left(\frac{n(n-1)}2\right)^2.
\]
Thus familiar power-sum formulas are instances of a systematic discrete integration method.

\section{The same coefficients form a probability distribution}

Let \(X\) be the number of selected elements when each of \(n\) labels is independently included with probability \(p\). Then
\[
 \Pr(X=k)=\binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k}.
\]
The generating polynomial is
\[
 \mathbb E[z^X]=(1-p+pz)^n.
\]
Differentiating at \(z=1\) gives the factorial moments:
\[
 \mathbb E[X]=np,\qquad
 \mathbb E[X(X-1)]=n(n-1)p^2.
\]
Since \(X^2=X(X-1)+X\), we obtain
\[
 \boxed{\operatorname{Var}(X)=np(1-p).}
\]
For \(p=1/2\), the central binomial coefficients control the most likely subset sizes. Chebyshev's inequality already gives a quantitative concentration estimate:
\[
 \Pr\left(\left|\frac Xn-\frac12\right|\ge\varepsilon\right)
 \le\frac{1}{4n\varepsilon^2}.
\]
The binomial theorem therefore simultaneously normalizes a probability distribution and gives access to its fluctuations.

\section{From choosing subsets to assembling structures}

Subset counting asks us to label each element in one of two ways: selected or not selected. Other labelled structures can be assembled from components, with a generating function recording the number of ways to make each choice.

A permutation decomposes uniquely into disjoint cycles. The number of cycles of length \(k\) on a fixed \(k\)-element label set is \((k-1)!\), so the exponential generating function of one cycle is
\[
 C(z)=\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{(k-1)!}{k!}z^k
     =\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{z^k}{k}=-\log(1-z).
\]
A permutation is an \emph{unordered set of cycles}. The exponential formula for labelled structures therefore gives
\[
 P(z)=\exp(C(z))=\frac1{1-z}.
\]
If \(p_n\) denotes the number of permutations of \(n\) labels, the coefficient convention is
\(P(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}p_nz^n/n!\), so \(p_n=n!\), as expected.

The exponential formula can be checked directly in this case: expanding \(\exp(C(z))\) counts unordered collections of cycles, and the division by \(r!\) in the exponential removes the ordering of \(r\) components. The division by \(k!\) in the generating function records the relabelling of an individual size-\(k\) component. The algebra captures precisely the combinatorial symmetries involved.

Binomial coefficients begin as counts of subsets, but their usefulness comes from several compatible structures. Alternating signs implement inversion; finite differences provide discrete derivatives; generating functions record independent choices; and exponential generating functions account for the relabelling of assembled components. The same small counting identity has grown into a calculus for finite structures.
