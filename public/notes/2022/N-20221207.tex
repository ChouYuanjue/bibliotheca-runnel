\notechapter{N-20221207}{Sumset Growth, Boolean Coordinates, and Polynomial Counting}

A finite set can carry two very different kinds of algebra. If its elements are integers, adding two of them produces a sumset whose size reflects additive structure. If its elements are subsets of a fixed universe, taking symmetric difference produces a vector space over \(\mathbb F_2\). Both settings transform set manipulation into algebra, but they make distinct predictions about how quickly a set grows under repeated operations.\footnote{For additive-combinatorial bounds and their polynomial proofs, see Tao and Vu, \emph{Additive Combinatorics}, and Alon, \emph{Combinatorial Nullstellensatz}, \url{https://www.math.tau.ac.il/~nogaa/PDFS/comb_null.pdf}.}

\section{A sharp extremal bound from sums and differences}

Let \(A=\{a_1<\cdots<a_k\}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,2021\}\), and define
\[
 S=A+A=\{a+b:a,b\in A\},\qquad
 T=A-A=\{a-b:a,b\in A,\ a\ge b\}.
\]
Suppose \(S\cap T=\varnothing\). The \(2k-1\) sums
\[
 2a_1<a_1+a_2<2a_2<\cdots<a_{k-1}+a_k<2a_k
\]
are distinct. Thus \(|S|\ge2k-1\). The \(k\) nonnegative differences
\[
 0<a_2-a_1<\cdots<a_k-a_1
\]
are also distinct, giving \(|T|\ge k\). As the two sets are disjoint and lie in \(\{0,\ldots,2a_k\}\),
\[
 3k-1\le|S\cup T|\le2a_k+1\le4043.
\]
Therefore \(k\le1348\).

The bound is attained:
\[
 A=\{674,675,\ldots,2021\}.
\]
Its nonnegative difference set is \(\{0,\ldots,1347\}\), while its sumset is \(\{1348,\ldots,4042\}\). They are disjoint, and \(|A|=1348\).

The calculation is instructive because every inequality is sharp at the same configuration. The extremal set is a long arithmetic progression. Its sumset is as small as an integer sumset can be, and its large starting point separates the sums from the differences.

\section{Why arithmetic progressions have minimal sumset growth}

For any two finite nonempty subsets \(A,B\subset\mathbb Z\), write \(A=\{a_1<\cdots<a_r\}\) and \(B=\{b_1<\cdots<b_s\}\). The chain
\[
 a_1+b_1<a_2+b_1<\cdots<a_r+b_1
 <a_r+b_2<\cdots<a_r+b_s
\]
contains \(r+s-1\) distinct elements of \(A+B\), so
\[
 \boxed{|A+B|\ge|A|+|B|-1.}
\]
For arithmetic progressions with the same positive common difference, equality holds.

The inequality has a geometric interpretation: adding a second independent choice usually expands a set. When equality holds, the choices are aligned so that many distinct pairs yield the same sum. More elaborate inverse theorems in additive combinatorics ask whether small \(|A+A|\) forces a set to resemble a generalized arithmetic progression. The extremal example above is the simplest visible case of that question.

\section{The finite-field version is Cauchy--Davenport}

Over \(\mathbb F_p\), where \(p\) is prime, addition wraps around. The correct bound for nonempty \(A,B\subseteq\mathbb F_p\) is
\[
 \boxed{|A+B|\ge\min(p,|A|+|B|-1).}
\]
This is the Cauchy--Davenport theorem. The appearance of \(p\) reflects the impossibility of a sumset containing more than the entire field.

There is a striking polynomial proof of the essential case \(|A|+|B|-2<p\). Put \(r=|A|\), \(s=|B|\), and suppose for contradiction that \(|A+B|\le r+s-2\). Choose a set \(C\supseteq A+B\) of exactly \(r+s-2\) field elements, and form
\[
 P(X,Y)=\prod_{c\in C}(X+Y-c).
\]
The polynomial vanishes on every point of \(A\times B\). Yet the coefficient of \(X^{r-1}Y^{s-1}\) is
\[
 [X^{r-1}Y^{s-1}]P=\binom{r+s-2}{r-1},
\]
which is nonzero modulo \(p\), since \(r+s-2<p\).

The contradiction comes from polynomial interpolation. For any polynomial \(P\) of total degree at most \(r+s-2\), the coefficient above equals
\[
 \sum_{\substack{a\in A\\b\in B}}
 \frac{P(a,b)}
 {\displaystyle
  \prod_{a'\in A\setminus\{a\}}(a-a')
  \prod_{b'\in B\setminus\{b\}}(b-b')}.
\]
To see this, apply the one-variable Lagrange interpolation formula first in \(X\) and then in \(Y\); terms with total degree below \(r+s-2\) cannot contribute to the requested bidegree. Every summand on the right is zero because \(P\) vanishes on the grid, contradicting the nonzero binomial coefficient.

If \(r+s-1\ge p\), choose smaller nonempty subsets \(A'\subseteq A\), \(B'\subseteq B\) with \(|A'|+|B'|-1=p\), and apply the preceding argument to show \(A'+B'=\mathbb F_p\). This establishes the general bound.

An additive-combinatorial inequality has become a statement about the coefficient of a polynomial. The fields \(\mathbb Z\) and \(\mathbb F_p\) behave differently, but the same cardinality growth has an algebraic explanation in both.

\section[Subsets as vectors over F2]{Subsets of a universe are vectors over \(\mathbb F_2\)}

Now let \(U=\{1,\ldots,n\}\), and associate to each \(A\subseteq U\) its indicator vector
\[
 \mathbf1_A=(\mathbf1_{1\in A},\ldots,\mathbf1_{n\in A})\in\mathbb F_2^n.
\]
Under this encoding, symmetric difference becomes vector addition:
\[
 \mathbf1_{A\triangle B}=\mathbf1_A+\mathbf1_B\pmod2.
\]
Indeed an element belongs to \(A\triangle B\) precisely when it belongs to exactly one of \(A,B\).

The collection \(\mathcal P(U)\) is therefore an \(n\)-dimensional vector space over \(\mathbb F_2\). It has \(2^n\) elements, and an independent family of \(r\) subsets generates exactly \(2^r\) distinct symmetric differences.

For example, let \(U=\{1,2,3\}\) and take \(A=\{1,2\}\), \(B=\{2,3\}\). Then
\[
 A\triangle B=\{1,3\},\qquad
 \langle A,B\rangle_{\triangle}
   =\{\varnothing,A,B,\{1,3\}\}.
\]
Only four distinct subsets appear because the two indicator vectors generate a two-dimensional subspace.

Unlike ordinary sumsets in the integers, a vector subspace can be perfectly stable under addition: \(V+V=V\). A small doubling set may therefore signal algebraic closure rather than the geometry of a progression, depending on the ambient group.

\section{Venn diagrams are coordinate decompositions}

Given \(m\) subsets \(A_1,\ldots,A_m\) of \(U\), each element \(u\in U\) has a membership signature
\[
 \sigma(u)=(\mathbf1_{u\in A_1},\ldots,\mathbf1_{u\in A_m})
 \in\{0,1\}^m.
\]
The \(2^m\) possible signatures partition \(U\) into disjoint Boolean atoms. Every expression built from unions, intersections and complements is a union of some of these atoms.

This gives a rigorous algebraic alternative to visually reading a complicated Venn diagram. For example,
\[
 A\setminus(B\cup C)=A\cap B^c\cap C^c
\]
selects exactly the signature \((1,0,0)\). The proof is a one-line statement about membership coordinates.

There is also a useful distinction between two operations. Intersection acts like coordinatewise multiplication of indicator vectors, while symmetric difference acts like addition modulo two. These are precisely the ring operations of a Boolean ring. Its algebra is different from integer addition, but both arise from asking which combinations of finite objects preserve information and which combinations force growth.
