\notechapter{N-20220810}{Three Elementary Inequalities and Their Geometric Meaning}

Cauchy--Schwarz, AM--GM and the triangle inequality appear in almost every early inequality exercise. Their proofs have different mechanisms: positivity of a square, concavity of logarithm, and the geometry of a norm. The presentation follows the standard inequality toolkit of Hardy, Littlewood and Pólya's \emph{Inequalities}, with the vector interpretation aligned with MIT 18.06.\footnote{\url{https://web.mit.edu/18.06/www/index.shtml}}

\section{Squares give sharp inequalities and equality cases}

For real \(a,b\), nonnegativity of \((a-b)^2\) gives
\[
 a^2+b^2\ge2ab,
\]
with equality if and only if \(a=b\). Adding this identity over pairs yields
\[
 2(a^2+b^2+c^2-ab-bc-ca)
 =(a-b)^2+(b-c)^2+(c-a)^2\ge0.
\]
Equality holds precisely at \(a=b=c\). This pattern is called a sum-of-squares certificate: it proves the inequality and simultaneously explains when the deficit vanishes.

\section{Cauchy--Schwarz from the discriminant}

For vectors \(u,v\in\mathbb R^n\), the quadratic
\[
 q(t)=\|u-tv\|^2
 =\|v\|^2t^2-2\langle u,v\rangle t+\|u\|^2
\]
is nonnegative for every real \(t\). When \(v\ne0\), its discriminant must be nonpositive, so
\[
 \langle u,v\rangle^2\le\|u\|^2\|v\|^2.
\]
When \(v=0\), both sides vanish. Equality for nonzero \(v\) occurs exactly when \(u=tv\) for some scalar \(t\). The equality condition is a dependence relation, not merely a coincidence of numbers.

For \(a_i>0\), substitute
\[
 u_i=\frac{x_i}{\sqrt{a_i}},\qquad v_i=\sqrt{a_i}.
\]
Cauchy then becomes its Engel form:
\[
 \boxed{\sum_i\frac{x_i^2}{a_i}\ge
 \frac{(\sum_i x_i)^2}{\sum_i a_i}.}
\]
Equality holds when \(x_i/a_i\) is independent of \(i\).

\begin{example}
For \(a,b,c>0\), applying Engel with denominators \(b+c,c+a,a+b\) gives
\[
 \frac{a^2}{b+c}+\frac{b^2}{c+a}+\frac{c^2}{a+b}
 \ge\frac{(a+b+c)^2}{2(a+b+c)}
 =\frac{a+b+c}{2}.
\]
Equality requires \(a/(b+c)=b/(c+a)=c/(a+b)\), equivalent in this positive setting to \(a=b=c\).
\end{example}

\section{The triangle inequality is the norm form of Cauchy}

For Euclidean vectors,
\[
 \|u+v\|^2=\|u\|^2+2\langle u,v\rangle+\|v\|^2
 \le(\|u\|+\|v\|)^2,
\]
so \(\|u+v\|\le\|u\|+\|v\|\). In \(\mathbb R\) this is \(|a+b|\le|a|+|b|\); substituting \(u=a-b\), \(v=b-c\) gives
\[
 |a-c|\le|a-b|+|b-c|.
\]
Rearranging the triangle inequality in both directions gives the reverse form
\[
 \bigl|\|u\|-\|v\|\bigr|\le\|u-v\|.
\]

In Euclidean space, equality in the forward triangle inequality occurs when the nonzero vectors point in the same direction. In a general normed space equality can also occur for other configurations; for example \(\|(1,0)+(0,1)\|_1=2=\|(1,0)\|_1+\|(0,1)\|_1\). This shows why the equality criterion needs to mention the norm.

\section{AM--GM follows from concavity}

For positive \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\), the second derivative of the logarithm is
\[
 (\log x)''=-x^{-2}<0,
\]
so \(\log\) is strictly concave. Jensen's inequality gives
\[
 \log\left(\frac{x_1+\cdots+x_n}{n}\right)
 \ge\frac1n\sum_i\log x_i
 =\log\left(\prod_i x_i\right)^{1/n}.
\]
Exponentiation yields
\[
 \boxed{\frac{x_1+\cdots+x_n}{n}\ge
  (x_1\cdots x_n)^{1/n}.}
\]
Strict concavity forces equality exactly when all \(x_i\) are equal. The formula extends to nonnegative variables by continuity; when any variable is zero, the right-hand side is zero, and equality requires all variables to be zero.

For \(x>0\), apply this to \(x\) and \(1/x\):
\[
 x+\frac1x\ge2,
\]
with equality at \(x=1\). For \(x,y,z>0\), the product of \(x/y\), \(y/z\), \(z/x\) is \(1\), so
\[
 \frac{x}{y}+\frac{y}{z}+\frac{z}{x}\ge3,
\]
with equality at \(x=y=z\).

\section{Coordinate geometry is an inner-product calculation}

Let \(A=(x_1,y_1)\), \(B=(x_2,y_2)\). The distance formula is
\[
 |AB|^2=(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2
 =\langle B-A,B-A\rangle.
\]
For direction vectors \(u,v\), perpendicularity is the invariant relation
\(\langle u,v\rangle=0\). When both lines have finite slopes \(m_1,m_2\), direction vectors may be chosen as \((1,m_1)\), \((1,m_2)\), so perpendicularity becomes \(1+m_1m_2=0\).

The slope rule \(m_1m_2=-1\) does not cover vertical lines, but the dot-product rule does. Likewise, checking parallelism via a determinant \(\det[u\ v]=0\) works whether the slopes exist or not.

\section{A matrix version of the same square argument}

For a real symmetric matrix \(A\), the condition
\[
 x^TAx\ge0\qquad\text{for all }x
\]
defines positive semidefiniteness, denoted \(A\succeq0\). For
\[
 A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\b&c\end{pmatrix},
\]
the exact criterion is
\[
 a\ge0,\quad c\ge0,\quad ac-b^2\ge0.
\]
If \(a>0\), completing a square gives
\[
 ax^2+2bxy+cy^2
 =a(x+(b/a)y)^2+(c-b^2/a)y^2;
\]
the case \(a=0\) requires \(b=0\) and \(c\ge0\). This is the two-dimensional analogue of proving an inequality by writing a sum of squares.

The three elementary methods now have distinct mechanisms: Cauchy is controlled by a nonnegative quadratic and linear dependence; AM--GM is controlled by concavity and equality of inputs; the triangle inequality describes metric geometry. Choosing the method means recognizing which mathematical structure the problem actually supplies.
